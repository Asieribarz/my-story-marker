# The Cold Lamp

*An adventure in 20 pages.*


## 1. The Dark on the Water

The packet put Mara Vell ashore at Ossary on the third day of the storm season, and she knew before her boots were dry that something on the Banks had gone wrong.

It was the quay that told her. In storm season every boat in the archipelago lies to her moorings and every crew stands about the fish quay with nothing to do but argue over who is owed what, and that much was ordinary. What was not ordinary was that the arguing stopped as she came along the breakwater, and started up again behind her, lower.

She went to the head of the slip, where the bell stands on its post, and put her back to the wind to look east.

Nothing out there. Two miles of open channel, the outer Banks beyond it, and on the far rock a white tower eighty feet up from its landing with no light on the top of it.

"Three nights," a voice said behind her. "Going on four."

Halloran Keeve came down the slip without hurrying, in the way of a man who has decided in advance not to be much help. Wool cap. The right hand with its two missing fingers pushed down into his coat.

"Three nights dark?" Mara said.

"Aye."

"And nobody has gone out to it?"

"The Weathering has been foul since Tuesday, lass. Slack water comes twice a day and lasts under the hour, and there is not a crew on this quay who will spend their hour crossing two miles of open ground for a rock that has stopped paying them."

"It has not stopped paying. My brother is on it."

Halloran looked at the water rather than at her. "Weather makes its own law," he said. "There is no arguing with a sea that does not want you in it."

"Is there arguing with a boat?"

He did not answer that.

So she went down the quay and asked anyway, from the first mooring to the last: a crossing, at the next slack, any price she could raise. She got civility from some of them and the weather from the rest, and from one man a long look at her oilskin coat, two sizes large, and the question of whether she could swim.

By the time she came back to the bell she had asked every boat in Ossary Harbour, and not one of them would take her out to it.

The harbour office was a stone room with one window and the keepers' roll nailed inside the door, where it had hung since before Mara was born. Every light in the Banks was on it: every keeper, every relief, every date of entry and leaving, in a clerk's copperplate that had not varied in thirty years.

She found the Cold Lamp two-thirds of the way down.

Her brother's name had been struck out. One line, ruled against something straight, and written over the top of it in a different ink, a name she did not know.

She stood with her thumb under the entry longer than she needed to read it. The ink over the strike was newer than the ink under it. Not by much. Days, perhaps.

"Who is this?" she said.

Halloran had come in behind her and stopped just inside the door, where a man stands when he intends to leave soon.

"That is the roll," he said. "The roll says what it says."

"Who wrote it?"

"Where did a girl off the Ossary packet learn to read a keepers' roll?"

"Where did the relief go?"

He looked at the roll and not at her. "There is no weather in a question," he said. "Be careful how many of them you ask on this quay. There are men here who are owed money and men who owe it, and neither sort likes being asked things."

"Which sort are you?"

"I am a man who has a boat to see to."

Mara put her finger on the newer ink and held it there until he looked. Up close the letters were cramped, as though the hand had been in a hurry, or had not wanted to be recognised.

"Say it," she said.

Halloran looked at the name, and at the window, and at the quay beyond the door, where thirty men stood about in the rain with nothing to do but listen.

"No," he said. "Not in this room, lass, and not on this quay, and not for the price of a crossing either."

He went out into the rain. Mara stayed where she was with her thumb on the roll, reading the name over and over and getting nothing out of it: two words of ink she could not lift, set over her brother in a hurried hand. The one man on the quay who knew what they meant had walked out of the room rather than say them aloud.

The warden of Gannet Isle kept a table at the back of the harbour office through storm season, because storm season was when the wreck law earned. She was there at noon with her grey coat buttoned to the throat and her gloves still on, and a ledger squared up under her left forearm.

"Ester Quill," she said, before Mara had asked. "You are the sister."

"You know my brother?"

"I know every keeper on the Banks. It is in the article. The warden of the nearest island takes the inventory when a light is left." Quill turned the ledger a little, so that it faced neither of them. "Sit down. You have come a long way to be told something short."

Mara did not sit.

"His name is struck off the roll," she said.

"It is."

"Who struck it?"

"I did. On the eleventh." Quill's voice did not rise at any point in the sentence. "A keeper who does not answer the bell three days running is entered as drowned, and the light is put under the nearest warden until the service sends a relief. Article nine. You may read it. Most people prefer to be told."

"Told by whom?"

"By me, as it happens." The gloved hands folded. "I am sorry. You will want it to be more complicated than it is, and it is not. The Weathering is the deepest ground in the archipelago and it took a young man who was out on it in a season when nobody should be. That is the whole of it."

"There was a name written over his."

Something went across the warden's face, so briefly that afterwards Mara could not have sworn to it.

"The relief," Quill said. "Naturally."

"What is the relief's name?"

"There is a packet out on Friday's tide," said Quill, "and I will pay your passage home on it. That is not charity, it is arithmetic. A girl asking questions on this quay in storm season costs me more than a berth does." She drew the ledger back under her arm. "Take the passage. Ask nothing further between now and Friday, and I will see there is money in your hand at the other end as well."

Mara found him at the third mooring with the boat's papers spread on a thwart under a scrap of tarpaulin, doing sums that were not coming out.

"How much for the crossing?" she said.

"There is no crossing."

"How much if there were?"

Halloran put his two-fingered hand flat on the papers to keep the wind off them. "You are not the first to ask me that this week," he said, "and I gave the others the weather, and the weather is still true. A boat is worth nothing on the bottom of the Weathering."

"Is a boat worth anything in a ledger?"

He stopped.

She had not meant it cruelly, and after a moment he seemed to take it that way. He lifted the tarpaulin so that she could see the papers under it: the boat's registry, and clipped to the registry a note of hand with an amount at the foot of it and a date eleven days past.

"Forty-two on the note," he said, "and the note is hers. She bought it off the chandler in the spring for less than it says, which is the way of it. Every second boat on this quay has a paper like that in the warden's ledger. She does not ask for the money. She asks for the hour, and the mooring, and where you were, and who you carried, and a man says aye because the alternative is a boat sold out from under him in storm season."

"She asked you for an hour this week?"

"She asked all of us not to spend one crossing east." He folded the papers. "That is not an order. An order can be shown to somebody."

"Then show her nothing," Mara said. "Take the money instead. What I have is thirty-one and the coat."

"Keep the coat, lass."

"Is that a yes?"

Halloran looked east, where there was still no light, and did the sum one more time, and it came out the way it had been coming out all week.

"Slack water is at four," he said. "We will have under the hour of it and the fourth day of the storm behind us the whole way over. Be at the slip with the tide and nothing in your hands you cannot afford to let go of."


## 2. The Cold Lamp

Slack water came at four, and it came the way it always comes in the Weathering: the sea lay down flat and wrong-feeling, as though it had been told to.

Halloran had the sail up before Mara had the warp off the ring. He did not talk on the way out past the breakwater, and she was glad of it, because talking would have meant looking at him, and looking at him would have meant looking at the water on either side.

A mile out, she made herself do it anyway.

Green going to black. No bottom in it, no weed, no shadow of ground, nothing for the eye to stand on. Under the middle mile of the Weathering lies the deepest ground in the archipelago, and she had known that since she was six years old, and she had crossed it perhaps nine times in her life, and every one of those nine times she had found something on the far side to look at instead.

"How much of the hour is gone?" she said.

"Half."

"Will it do?"

"It will do if you stop asking me and start on the bailer."

She bailed. The work helped. Fifty minutes out the wind came round in the space of three breaths, the way it does on the fourth day, and the flat water stood up in short steep seas that came from two directions and met under the boat.

"That is the slack away," Halloran said. "Look behind you, lass."

She looked.

Where they had come from, the channel was going white. Not breaking in one place and then another, but the whole two miles of it turning over at once, all the way back to the breakwater, which was already too small to make out.

"Put me about," she said, "and we would never get in."

"No."

"And the next slack?"

"Is at four tomorrow, and in this weather I would not give you odds on it." He held the tiller with the two-fingered hand and did not look pleased about any of it. "You have your crossing. There is no undoing a crossing once the water shuts behind it. Whatever is on that rock, lass, you are on it with them until the sea says otherwise."

They came in on the landing at Calder Rock in the last of the light, which on the fourth day of a storm season is no light at all, and Halloran put the boat alongside on the third attempt with the sea working under the stones the whole time.

There is no soil on Calder Rock. Nothing grows on it and nothing settles on it. The tower stands eighty feet up from the landing, white in daylight and grey in this, and everything about the place is loud: the sea going in under the landing and coming out again, the wind working at the glass up top, and a low note in the stone itself that Mara felt through her boots before she heard it.

"I will lie here an hour," Halloran said, "and then I will lie somewhere out of this swell. Do not be longer than the hour."

"Where would you go?"

"That is my business."

She went up. A hundred and nine steps of iron stair, in three flights, with the rail cold enough to take the skin off a wet hand. She counted them because her brother had counted them to her once, in a kitchen at Ossary, with the number offered as though it were a wonder rather than a chore.

At the top of the stair was the door into the tower, and the door was not barred.

That stopped her longer than anything had stopped her all day. A keeper bars the door at dusk. It is the first thing in the book and the last thing in the hand: you bar it against the weather if not against anybody else, and on the fourth day of the season the weather is reason enough twice over.

The door was not barred and the door was wet. Not spray-wet. Wet in a track, in and up, where somebody had come through it with the sea still running off them.

Mara put the lamp down on the sill and looked at the track going away from her into the tower, and the marks in it were prints, and they were going up, and they were smaller than her own.

The prints went up two flights and stopped at the service room, and there was nobody in the service room.

Nobody in the watch room above it. Nobody in the bunk room below. The bunk was made up square, the way a keeper makes it up when he goes off watch expecting to come back to it, and there was a mug on the shelf with a finger of cold tea in the bottom and a skin across the top that was days old rather than hours.

Mara stood in the middle of it with her lantern and made herself do the thing properly, room by room, because doing it properly was better than the alternative.

Nothing was broken. Nothing was overturned. No blood, no note, no bag packed and left. It was not a place anybody had been taken out of. It was a place somebody had walked out of, in their own time, and had not come back to.

She went up the last flight to the lantern room.

The lens stood in its brass frame in the middle of the floor, the size of a water butt, and it was the only clean thing left on the Rock. Somebody had wiped it down. Somebody had squared the frame up and set the whole assembly true, the way you leave a thing you intend to come back to and do not intend anybody else to touch.

Under it was the reservoir.

Mara put her hand flat on the reservoir the way you put your hand on a kettle, which is a stupid thing to do and everybody does it. Then she took the cap off and looked in, and put the lantern over it, and looked again.

Dry. Not low. Dry to the brass, with the wick standing up out of an empty well, and no smell of oil in the room at all.

The whole tower was cold. The stair was cold and the bunk was cold and the mug was cold. But she had climbed a hundred and nine steps telling herself there would be some reason, and here was the reason and it was no reason at all: the lamp was cold.

The log was in the watch room on its shelf, and it had not been hidden, which Mara thought about for some time afterwards.

A keeper's log is not a diary. It is weather, hours, oil drawn, wicks trimmed, vessels sighted, bells heard, one line each and the date in the margin. Her brother's hand filled it from the turn of the month, small and upright and slow, and she could hear him in it the way you hear somebody in a shopping list.

Bells heard was the column she came back to.

On the first: three strokes on Gannet, at dusk.

She read the line four times. Three strokes is a death. It is the one signal in the Banks that nobody mistakes and nobody rings twice, and it carries the length of the archipelago on a still evening, which is why it is rung at dusk. Somewhere on Gannet Isle, on the first of the month, a bell had been rung for a death.

The next line was in the same upright hand.

On the first, later: rung for Vell. Asked by whistle who is dead. No answer from Gannet.

Then the second. Then the third. Oil drawn, wicks trimmed, a lugger sighted standing off the Stair without lights, which he had underlined. On the eighth he had written the word asked twice and struck it out both times. On the ninth he had written nothing at all, and there were no lines after that, and the book had been left open on the shelf at the page.

Halloran came up behind her with the wind coming up the stair after him and stood looking at the empty lantern room rather than at the book.

"Eight days," Mara said.

"Eight days what, lass?"

"They rang him dead on the first." She put her finger on the line so that the number showed. "He wrote in this until the ninth. Somebody on Gannet rang a death bell for my brother eight days before there was anything to ring it for."


## 3. Wreck Law

The lugger came alongside the landing at first light, and it came alongside better than the boat that brought her had, which told Mara something about how often it had been done.

She had the tower door shut and herself three flights up before the man was off the stones. Then she lay along the iron stair in the dark with her face at the gap under the rail and let him come up past her.

He went by close enough to touch. Twenty-two or thereabouts, salt-burned across the cheekbones, a salvage hook hanging at his belt with an edge on it the work did not need, and a good coat that sat wrong across the shoulders, as though it had been cut for a wider man. He had a tally book under his arm and he was already talking to it.

"Inventory of the store at Calder, the thirteenth," he said. "Under article four. Goods in a light left unkept pass to the warden of the nearest island until claimed. That is the order."

He said the last of it the way a man says a thing he has been given to say.

Then he went down to the oil store, and Mara heard him counting.

He counted aloud. He counted the way a careful man counts, in threes, and at the end of the row he went back and did it again, and the second count came out where the first had.

"Nineteen," he said.

There was a long pause, with the sea going in under the landing.

"Nineteen," said Dov Ashe again. "Book says thirty."

Mara lay on the stair with the cold coming up through her coat and did the sum for him. Thirty casks in the book, nineteen on the floor, and a reservoir dry to the brass.

"Eleven," Ashe said. He said it a third time, more quietly, as though the number were a thing he had been hoping not to find. "Eleven short."

She came off the lugger at Gannet under a tarpaulin and a coil of warp, and walked up through a village where the gate posts were masts and the roofs were hatch covers and the yard wall was somebody's deck.

The lamp-wright's shed was the last building before the warden's house. Mara went in because the door was open and because a woman inside was working a brass fitting in a vice with a rag and a pot of something, and because the fitting was a lamp fitting.

"Do not touch the tray," the woman said, without turning round. "Move your hand. Thank you."

"You are Carrow."

"Nell Carrow. Which makes you the sister, because there is nobody else new on this island in storm season and you have his chin." She set the rag down. "You have been out to the Rock."

"Have you?"

Nell Carrow looked at her for a while. She had a lamp-wright's apron with glass dust worked into the seams, goggles pushed up on her forehead and left there, and burn scars on both thumbs, old and even.

"On the seventh," she said. "I went out on the seventh to trim a wick that did not need trimming, and he would not let me up past the service room, and I have thought about that every day since. Ask me the question."

"Is he dead?"

"No."

Mara put her hand on the bench because the bench was there.

"Then where -"

"That is the second question and you will not like the first answer well enough to want it." Nell picked up the fitting and put it down again. "Your brother put that light out. Not a fault, not a failure, not weather. He stripped the reservoir and he carried the oil off the Rock himself. In the nine days since, two vessels have come onto the Drowned Stair in the dark, and what came off the second of them is stacked in the yard you walked past to get here."

The shed was very quiet. Somewhere below, the sea took a run at the wreck-timber wall and went away again.

"So," Nell said. "You came out here to find a drowned boy and there is not one to find. When you get to him, whose side are you going to be standing on?"

The yard took up the whole slope behind the wall, and everything in it had belonged to somebody else first. Spars in stacks by length. Cordage under tarpaulin. A companion ladder leaning against a chicken house. At the top, under a lean-to with a proper roof on it, the barrels.

"Eleven," Mara said.

"Count them yourself," said Nell. "Do not take my word and do not move them, the chocks are wrong."

Eleven casks, in two rows, with the service mark burned into the head of each one: the anchor and the lamp, and under it CALDER.

"Whale-oil," Nell said. "Keeper's issue. It does not go anywhere but into a reservoir and it is not sold on this island or any other, because every cask in the Banks is marked and every mark is in a book."

"Which book?"

Nell pointed with her chin at the lean-to wall, where a tin box was screwed to a post at shoulder height, and in the tin box was the yard ledger, because a ledger under a roof is worth more than a ledger in a house that burns.

Mara had it open on the barrel head in under a minute.

Wreck goods, entered by line, each with its article, its finder, and its date. She went down the page with her thumb until the anchor and the lamp came up under it.

Eleven casks, whale-oil, keeper's issue, Calder. Taken up on the flats. Held under article four, nine days to a living claim.

She read the entry twice. Then she read the date at the head of it, and put her thumb on that, and held it there, and felt the whole shape of the thing come round underneath her like a boat coming about.

"Nell," she said. "What day did the Lamp go dark?"

"The eighth. Everybody on this island knows the eighth."

"Then look at this."

Nell came and looked, and did not say anything for a moment, which from her was a great deal.

The casks had been entered into the yard ledger as wreck goods on the fifth. Three days before there was any wreck, three days before the reservoir was dry, three days before the Cold Lamp went out.

"Article four requires the entry to be made when the goods are taken up," said the warden, from the gate of the lean-to. "Not when they are found. Not when they are sold. When they are taken up. You are reading it correctly."

Mara did not turn round straight away. There was a particular pleasure in not turning round, and she thought she was owed it.

"You entered them three days early."

"I entered them on the day they came into my yard." Ester Quill came up the slope with her ledger under her left arm, gloved, buttoned, entirely dry in weather that had soaked everything else on the island. "Which is a different offence, and a smaller one, and one I would have to be asked about by somebody with standing to ask."

"Whose standing?"

"The service's. The owner's. Not a girl off the Ossary packet." She stopped on the other side of the barrel head. "Put the ledger back in its box, please. That is not a threat. The box keeps it dry and I am fond of it."

"You had the oil off that Rock before the light went out."

"I had eleven casks entered on the fifth, and you have a date and no more than a date." Quill's voice did not lift. "Shall I tell you what a date is worth in an admiralty room, against a warden of eleven years with a clean book? It is worth the cost of getting there. Which brings us to the part you will want to hear."

"Which part?"

"Your brother is alive." She let that sit exactly as long as it needed to. "You suspected it, and I am confirming it, and I am the only person on these Banks who will confirm it to you in writing. There is a berth on Friday's packet. Take it, and go home, and I will see he is entered on the roll as relieved rather than as a man who stripped a keeper's lamp and let two vessels onto the Stair behind him. He will have a name to come home to. That is my offer, and I make it once."

Mara looked at the entry on the barrel head, and at the gloves, and at the door of the yard forty feet behind the warden and standing open.

Then she took hold of the page and tore it out along the stitching, and went through the gap at the run.


## 4. The Drowned Stair

He caught her on the flats below the yard, where the ground is all channels and soft going and there is nowhere to run that is not slower than walking. He had the hook off his belt before he reached her, and then he stood three feet away and did not use it, and after a moment he put it back.

"You are quick over bad ground," said Dov Ashe. "Give me the page."

"Whose page?"

"That is the second time this week I have been asked a question I am not to answer." He looked back up the slope at the gate. Nobody had come through it yet. "Nineteen casks," he said. "Thirty in the book."

"You counted it twice."

"I count everything twice. I counted the Stair twice, and both times there was a light standing off it with no business being there, and both times I was told what I had seen." His jaw worked. "I am not - "

He stopped. He was a man who could not finish a sentence about himself.

"Ask me what you came out here to ask," he said.

"Who rang the bell?"

"She did. On the first, at dusk, from the post outside her own door, three strokes, for Vell." He said it the way he had said the article: flat, a thing given him to carry. "A keeper who is dead is a keeper the roll can be closed on. A light with no keeper passes to the warden of the nearest island. She rang it eight days before there was anything to ring, and I stood in the yard and heard it and said nothing, because that is the order."

The tide was coming across the flats behind him in a thin sheet, the way it does before it comes properly.

"And the name written over his?"

"Mine," said Ashe. "Relief keeper of the Cold Lamp. I have never been up that tower except to count what is in it."

He stood aside out of the channel. "There is a punt drawn up at the end of the flats. It is hers, and the only thing on this island that will swim at half tide. Take it, and when she asks me I will have counted wrong."

Then he pointed east with the hook, out past the flats to where the reef ran away from Calder Rock in steps.

"He is on the Drowned Stair. He has been on it four days. And the tide is making."

The punt swam at half tide, as promised, and it took her the length of the channel with the flood pushing her along faster than she wanted to be pushed.

The Drowned Stair runs out from the foot of Calder Rock in steps, square enough at the edges that half the Banks will tell you it was cut and the other half will tell you where the quarry was. At low water the first four steps stand clear. Mara came onto it at half flood with two of them left, and the reef took the legs out from under the sea the way it always does, so that the loudest place in the archipelago went quiet around her.

He was sitting on the second step with a crate between his knees.

A hand taller than her and thinner than he had been. The Ossary mark at the shoulder of a jersey that had gone through at both elbows. An oil burn along the right forearm, shiny and new enough that she could date it to a night she could name.

"Tem," she said.

Her brother looked up, and the first thing he did, before anything else, was apologise.

"I know. I know what it looks like from where you are standing, and I want to say first that the fault in the arrangement is mine and I am not going to put it on anybody else, because if you work backward from the reservoir you arrive at me, every time, and I have done that arithmetic more often than you would think." He had not stood up. "I did not know you would come. I have been trying to work out, since the ninth, what I would say if somebody came, and I have had four days on this step and I still have not got it down to a length a person could listen to."

"Where is the lens?"

He put his hand flat on the crate. Inside it, packed in oakum, were the clockwork and the burner and the brass frame in pieces, each part wrapped separately, in the way a man wraps things he intends to put back.

"The lens is under the third step," he said, "lashed to the ringbolt, because the third step does not dry and nobody looks at what does not dry."

Mara looked down. The third step was gone under green water, and the water was over her brother's boots, and up his shins, and still coming.

"Get in the punt."

"I cannot do that yet, and I want to explain why, because without the explanation I am only a man who broke a light." Tem had both arms round the crate now. "The lamp was not failing. On the twenty-ninth I was directed to show it at four flashes to the minute instead of three. In writing. Article eleven, the warden may direct the character of a light in the interest of navigation. Four to the minute is the character of the Sound light, which stands eleven miles south of here over clean water, and a master who takes this rock for that one puts his helm up and comes straight onto this reef."

Mara stood in the water and let that arrive.

"They came on," she said.

"One did. On the second. I got a boat out and took nine men off her, and the cargo was entered in the yard before they were dry." He stopped. "I could not keep showing it. I could not show it right either, because the direction was in writing and I am an assistant with his hand on a wick. So I took out what makes it a light and put it where it could not be shown at all."

"And two have come on since. In the dark."

"Three men. I know the number. I have had four days on this step to know it." He said it without any defence in his voice at all. "A dark rock is not a lie, though. In the dark they stand off it. It is the light that brings them in."

"Get in the punt, Tem."

"Not without the mechanism."

"Then I leave you both."

"That is not -" He looked up at her, and something in him gave slightly. "You would not."

And then, from the post at the head of the landing on Calder Rock, two miles of quiet water away, the bell went.

One stroke. A pause. A second.

Two is a wreck. Somebody was on the Rock to be ringing it, which was a thing to think about afterwards. Mara turned in water that was over her knees and looked north into the dark, where there was no light on Calder and none to have warned anybody off it, and somewhere out there a hull was on the ground with men in the water round her, and the two of them stood on the Stair and listened to it.

There are two ways off a step like that. One is the punt, with the mechanism in the crate and the lens left where it is, and a lamp that cannot be made again inside a month. The other is down.

Mara had known the answer since the bell. What took the time was the arithmetic her body was doing without being asked: forty feet of green water off the edge of the reef, the old quarry channel under it, no bottom she would be able to see, nothing for the eye to stand on.

She took her coat off and gave it to her brother, because a coat two sizes large will drown you.

"Mara."

"Third step," she said. "Ringbolt. You said the lashing is three turns and a hitch?"

"Three and a half. Mara, I will go, it is my - "

"You cannot swim and I can. Hold the crate."

She did not give herself the second she would have used to think better of it. She went off the second step feet first with the knife in her fist, and the water took her in the way that water takes you in the first week of a storm season, which is not like cold but like being held.

Down. The green went out of it at a fathom and after that there was only the dark and the reef going past her hands, step, step, step, and the sound of her own heart arriving at somewhere behind her ears, and no bottom, and no end to it, and every one of the nineteen years she had spent refusing to do this lined up to tell her to turn round.

She did not turn round.

The ringbolt was where he said. The lashing was three turns and a half and the knife went through it on the second draw, and the lens came off the reef into her arms, dead-heavy and as awkward as a water butt, liftable at all only because the water carried half of it, and she went up the way you go up when there is no more air, which is without any dignity at all.

She came out on the second step on her knees with the lens in both arms and her brother's hands under her elbows, and she got one breath, and then another, and she was still holding it.

Out north, across two miles of black water, the bell had stopped ringing, and it did not start again.


## 5. Slack Water

Halloran had been lying in the lee of the Rock since the night before, which was why there had been somebody on Calder to ring the bell. A ketch had touched on the outer end of the reef in the dark and hung there twenty minutes and come off again on the flood, and he had rung two strokes and stopped when she swung clear, and he stood by her until her master had way on him and could steer.

"Two hours in that," he said, "off a lee shore, for a man I have never met."

"Will they pay it?"

"They will pay it. Standing by is salvage whether a man wants it to be or not, and her master wrote me a line for it before he would let me go."

They crossed the Weathering on the four o'clock slack with the lens chocked amidships in a nest of oakum and the crate lashed under the thwart, and this time Mara sat facing the water and looked at it the whole way.

Halfway over, Halloran started talking and did not stop for a mile.

He had it all: the forty-two on the note, and what the note had really been for, which was not money. He had been asked to keep boats off the east crossing in storm season and he had kept them off. He had told a girl on a quay that the weather made its own law when what he meant was that the warden did. He said it plainly, without a proverb anywhere in it, and when he had finished he said that he would give it in the same words to anybody who would write it down.

At Ossary he went up to the office ahead of her and paid the note off out of the salvage line, in front of three men who would talk about it, and took the boat's papers back into his own hand.

Then Mara laid the claim.

The clerk read it twice: eleven casks, whale-oil, keeper's issue, marked Calder, held in the Gannet yard under article four since the fifth. He got the book out and ran his finger down it and stopped.

"You are inside the nine days," he said. "By a day and a half. But this is a claim by a living owner, and you are not the owner of it. Keeper's issue is claimed by the keeper. I need his hand on this, on this paper, before the fourteenth is out."

They had him off the Stair on the morning slack and up the hundred and nine steps by eight o'clock, and he went up them slowly, the way a man goes up a stair he has been avoiding.

The tower was as she had left it. The bunk square. The mug on the shelf. The reservoir dry to the brass and the lantern room empty in the middle where the frame had stood.

Mara put the claim paper on the watch-room table and the pen beside it and did not say anything, because she had decided on the crossing that she was not going to argue him into it.

Tem read it all the way through twice. Then he read the line about the keeper.

"If I put my hand on this," he said, "I am the keeper of the Cold Lamp on the fifth, and on the eighth, and on every day between, which means the man who stripped it is the man who is claiming for it, and both of those are in the same sentence and they cannot be separated out afterwards by anybody, including me. I have been working out for four days whether there is a way to sign the first half."

"Is there?"

"No." He signed it. He did it in the small upright hand that filled the log, and he took his time over it, and when it was done he turned the paper round so that it faced her. "I will give the rest to the roll as well. All of it. The direction under article eleven, and the date on it, and what I did about it, and what came on the reef afterwards. I am not going to give the first part without the second, because the second part is mine."

"They will take your ticket."

"They will take my ticket." He said it the way he had said the number three on the Stair: without any defence in it at all. "I put a light out. There is no arrangement of the facts in which I did not."

He looked up the stair towards the empty lantern room, where the lens stood on the floor in its oakum with the sea light coming through it and laying a long band of it across the boards.

"So somebody had better tell me," he said, "who is going to light that. Because it is not going to be me."

The claim went in on the afternoon slack with two hours of the fourteenth left on it, and the eleven casks came back across on the second boat the following morning, still chocked the way they had been stacked, with the anchor and the lamp showing on every head.

Nell Carrow came with them, and she came with her own tools.

"Put it down there. Not there, there. Do not lift it by the frame, lift it by the plate under the frame, and if the plate is cracked I want to know before you tell me anything else about your day."

It took her from eight in the morning until the light went. She set the lens up on its bed and trued it with a straightedge and shims, and she rebuilt the mechanism out of the crate with the oakum laid out in the order it came off, and twice she stopped and took a thing apart again because it was a sixteenth out.

"You have done this before," Mara said.

"Once."

"On which light?"

Nell put the screwdriver down. "The Sound light. Nine years ago, when I was twenty and better with my hands than I was with anything else, and the keeper asked me to sit his watch and I sat it, and I fell asleep at four in the morning and it ran the reservoir out and it was dark for an hour and forty minutes before he woke. Nothing came on. Nothing at all came on, that night, in an hour and forty minutes of perfectly empty water." She picked the screwdriver up again. "I have not put my hand on an oath since. Hold the lamp higher."

At six she filled the reservoir out of the first cask.

At half past she stood in the lantern room with her burnt thumbs on the frame and said the keeper's words out loud, with Mara and a service clerk from Ossary standing behind her, and she got through them without any commentary at all, which for Nell Carrow was the largest thing she had ever done.

Then she lit it.

The lens took the flame and made it into something else entirely, and the first sweep went out over the Weathering, three flashes to the minute, and caught on its way past a lugger lying in the channel with no lights showing at all.

They took her off the lugger at slack water the next morning, and they took her under article four.

It was the neatness of it that Mara thought about afterwards. Not the arrest. The arithmetic. Eleven casks of keeper's issue entered in a yard ledger on the fifth, claimed by a living owner on the fourteenth, inside the nine days: which meant that from the fifteenth the entry was not a record of wreck goods at all but a record of goods held against a claim that had been made and allowed. Whatever the sea had put ashore on Gannet that season, the eleven casks had never been in it.

Ester Quill came up the landing at Calder with her coat buttoned and her gloves on and her ledger left behind her, which was the only thing about her that had changed.

"You will want to be told it was the bell," she said.

"Was it?"

"It was the date." She looked up the white tower, eighty feet of it, with the lantern room burning steadily at the top on three flashes to the minute. "Nine days is a generous term. It was written by men who assumed that a claim would have to cross water to be made, and I have had eleven years of watching it expire on people who could not get a boat. It did not occur to me that somebody would go under the water instead of across it."

"It would not."

"No." Quill considered her for a moment with something that was almost professional. "Article eleven will be the longer business. The direction was in my hand and it is in the service's hands now, and there were three men off the second vessel. You understand that your brother goes into that account as well."

"He put himself into it."

"So he did." She went past her down to the boat, and that was all of it.

The service sent no relief for eleven days. Mara took the watch because there was nobody else on the Rock to take it, and the lamp-wright showed her the trimming and the oil and the clock, and at dusk on the first night she went up the hundred and nine steps on her own.

She lit it at a quarter past. Then she sat down in the chair by the glass, facing east, over the deepest ground in the archipelago, and she stood her watch until morning, and she did not once look away from the water.
