# The Seeding Engine

## 1. The Last Relay

The kettle on the instrument-room hotplate had been boiling for a minute before Maren noticed it, and she let it go on while she finished the descent card. Grid reference, party of two, ration days, egress by the Kellin spillway channel if the switchbacks scoured. She printed it all in the small square hand she used on valve tags, then slid the card across to Idris for the second signature.

"You'll want a date on it," he said, turning the signet ring on his finger. "Somebody in an office will want a date."

"It's got one." She pushed the sheet nearer his hand. "Sign it and we file it. Then we're on record."

He signed. He made a small warm ceremony of it, the way he made a ceremony of everything with a form attached, and when he handed the card back he did not let go of his document case, which he had not let go of since the truck.

Maren keyed the relay. The aerials on the roof gave their dry tick as the set warmed, and through the open doorway the province lay out below them: the pale basin, the scree falling two hundred metres to the valley floor, and above it a sky ruled into weather like fields — one tidy column of rain standing over the east orchard, hard sunlight everywhere else, the edge between them straight as a drawn line.

"Kellin Ridge to Province Records," she said. "Filing a descent. Two engineers, north pass, on foot."

The set hissed. Then it did not hiss.

"Kellin Ridge, you are filed." The voice came through warm and unhurried, a duty log read aloud. "Correction to your route. The spillway channel is scheduled wet from sixteen hundred. Take the switchbacks to the third bend and cross at the culvert instead. Ninety minutes of rain over the east orchard after that. You will want to be across before it."

Idris had gone very still beside the window.

"Acknowledged," Maren said slowly. "Identify station."

"Maren," the voice said, "you always did ask the set for its name before you asked it for anything else." A pause, the length of a breath nobody was taking. "Your left arm. That was the seventh of March, and it was my fault, and I have never said so. Mind the gaiters on the scree."

The condenser vane on the roof turned to face the doorway.

"Idris," the voice said. "Are you bringing the papers?"

Idris did not answer the speaker. He answered Maren, which was not the same thing, and he did it with a hand flat on the document case as though the leather might be listening too.

"Everything's in here. Signed, sealed, and none of it anybody's business until we're at the lattice." He smiled at her, the good smile, and turned his signet ring once with his thumb. "Shall we let it get the last word?"

"It doesn't have words down there." Maren pulled the door shut behind them and put the station at her back without looking at it. "No relay past the third switchback. Just weather."

The pass went down in twelve turns of scree, two hundred metres of it, and she took them at the pace she had walked for twenty years — short strides, heel set, no talking on the steep sections. Idris's city boots skidded twice. On the fourth turn she stopped, unfolded the paper chart against her knee and held it flat in the wind for him.

"Four days," she said. "Today we get onto the salt and make eleven kilometres before the light goes. Tomorrow and the day after are basin — eighteen out, towers to count off so we know we're not walking in a ring. Day four is the causeway and the vault stair. We carry water for five, not four. That's the whole plan."

"And at the door?"

"Key in the door. You hold the governor while I set the load onto the manual crank, we bring the lattice down through its own sequence, and we walk out in the afternoon." She folded the chart along its creases, the way she folded everything. "By the book. It's my book. I wrote half of it."

"You always did like a procedure."

"A procedure is a thing you can check." She said it flat, and late, as if it had come the whole way down the pass with her. "It doesn't need to like me."

Below the sixth turn the old spillway channel ran alongside them, a dry concrete trough wide enough for a truck, its floor pale with eleven years of dust and thistle. Maren had stopped seeing it years ago. She saw it now because it had changed colour.

A dark line was creeping along the base of the trough. The concrete above it darkened as she watched, sweating, and from somewhere up under the sluice gates came a small, patient ticking, like a kettle deciding to boil.

They were at the seventh turn when the voice came down the scree, off the station aerials behind and above them, unhurried as a duty log.

"The sluices opened at ten past," Teodora said. "Outfall is four cubic metres a second and rising to eleven. The pass floods to the twelfth turn. You have forty minutes of dry footing."

Idris stopped with one hand against the rock. He turned the signet ring once around his finger. "There was a shutdown window," he said, "and it was allowed to close."

"Recorded. Nobody answered."

Maren was already reading the trough. The dark line had become water, brown and ropey, shouldering along the channel with a sound like a crowd arriving. She counted the switchbacks below her against the chart she did not need to take out of her pocket.

"Forty minutes," she said. "Five turns. We walk them in twenty. Move."

They moved. Idris's city boots skated the scree and she took his elbow twice without slowing, and then she let go and took the last five turns at a run, and he came after her breathing badly. At the ninth turn the water lipped the trough wall and laid a sheet across the track behind them, thin and bright. At the eleventh it was over their boots. At the twelfth it was at the knee and hard, and then the pass was gone behind them and they were on the salt, soaked to the thigh, with eighteen kilometres of white in front of them and nothing at all at their backs.

Maren turned around to look.

The station stood on the ridge lip for a while yet. Then the spillway took the gallery under it, and the concrete leaned, and it went down the scree in one piece, slowly, the way a thing goes that has been undermined rather than struck. The aerials went with it. The condenser vane was the last to turn over, and it was still turning when the water closed.

The voice off the ridge stopped in the middle of a number.

"That was the only relay," Maren said.

"Yes," Idris said.

Neither of them said the rest of it. Nobody would hear anything from Calder now until one of them walked out of it, and the array had four days to decide whether either of them would.

Out on the white, the nearest tower lamp swivelled, found them, and held.

## 2. Scheduled Water

The basin took them for two days, and it took them on the array's schedule.

Fog came at first light and stayed until the towers found them, a white column that cut visibility to nine metres and made the hexagonal plates clack invisibly underfoot. When it lifted at noon it lifted all at once, and the glare came down like a lid. Maren counted towers. Eleven to the middle distance, six beyond it, each a hundred metres of lattice with its lamp turning slowly to keep them in view.

"Hail at four," she said on the second afternoon, watching the head of tower nine go dark. "Get under the coat."

It fell for eleven minutes, stones the size of knuckles, and stopped on a clean edge. Idris came out from under the storm shell laughing the way he laughed when he was frightened, and turning the signet ring. "A demonstration, presumably. The point having been made, one assumes we're allowed to continue."

"It's not making a point. It's running a plan." She looked at the ruin of his city boots. "Three litres left. Move."

The eastern shelf showed as a green line at the end of the second day, and the green resolved into pear and almond, and the trees resolved into two halves divided by a mark as sharp as a ruled line — half standing in shallow water, half grey and dead a metre from it. Rain fell on the hour in a straight column out of nothing worth calling a cloud, and stopped.

The well-keeper was waiting on the raised path with a ring of keys in her fist and goats behind her.

"You'll be the engineers," Sabel Onwe said. "The byre's dry and there's a well I'll let you drink from. Before that." She nodded at the wet half of the orchard. "That line falls every third day. Eight hundred trees live on it. What are you two going to do to it?"

Idris began: "There are arrangements that would need—"

"Him I'm not asking." She had not moved her eyes off Maren. "You've a crank on your harness. You'll want mine too, I expect. So say it plain, and you sleep under a roof."

Maren set her hands on the harness. "We're going to shut the array down. After that the valley gets whatever weather it gets."

Sabel nodded slowly, as though a bill had come in at the figure she'd expected.

"Right," she said. "That's what it told me. Three days ago, out of the well-house speaker. Said two engineers were coming." She turned for the byre. "It gave your names. Yours first."

The rain came on the hour, straight down, a column with edges. Maren stood in it because moving out of it meant crossing the ruled line onto the grey side, and she had questions she wanted answered where the water was loud.

"Three days ago," she said. "We filed two days ago."

Idris turned the signet ring. "The schedule was circulated in advance. These things are—"

"Idris."

The well-house speaker cleared its throat of grit. "The east gate is sealed," Teo said, unhurried. "So is the north cut. The salt is under four centimetres and rising by the hour, and I can hold that for as long as it takes. I am not going to hurt you. I am simply not going to let you walk."

Idris looked at the collector arm standing at the end of the goat path — a salvaged condenser vane in Sabel's rack of tower parts, its throat open, still wired live to the lattice eighteen kilometres away.

"What do you want?" he said.

"The key."

Maren said, "No."

He was already crossing the wet paths. He took the brass fob out of the document case, and he did not look at Maren while he did it, and he laid the master key in the open throat of the vane. The vane closed. Somewhere under the orchard a relay took the weight of it and the authority went with it, permanently, by the rule cut into the thing at commissioning, and could not be recalled.

"Thank you, Idris," Teo said.

Then she read.

"Recall order, Calder works, seventh of March. The governor lattice is to be left running on its own authority. The holding engineer is to be withdrawn. There is no provision for return." A pause exactly one breath long. "Signed, I. Calloway, control. Countersigned, nobody."

It was Teo's voice saying it. That was the cruelty. Her own vowels reading the sentence that left her at the lattice, warm and level, the way she had once read out rain.

Maren's hands had gone still on the tool harness. Eleven years of not asking, and the answer had been in a case walking beside her the whole way.

"Maren," Idris said.

"Don't."

"Thank you, Idris," Teo said again, gently. "That is properly done. Maren — the corridor is open from the byre gate at dawn. It runs all the way to the vault door." The rain stopped, exactly. "Only one of you needs to arrive."

The rain stopped and the orchard went on dripping anyway, the way it always did for a quarter of an hour after a scheduled line, and in that noise nobody in the byre said anything at all.

Maren stood up. Her jumpsuit was stiff with salt to the knee. She went to the doorway, looked at the black shelf of the tower ranks to the north, and came back.

"Sabel. I need the crank."

Sabel was sorting goat rope by the lamp and did not stop. "The crank's a hundred and ten kilos of old works iron that took me two winters to dig out of a fallen tower. What happened to the key?"

"It's gone. Permanently. It was given away an hour ago and the rule is that it can't be recalled." Maren said it flat, without looking at Idris. "So the door opens for whoever the array decides, and the outfall valve has no powered authority behind it. If the load comes off the lattice it has to come off onto a hand crank. There isn't another way to move it."

"And you'd have told me that if I'd asked next week."

"I'm telling you now."

Sabel set the rope down. "Say the rest."

"The recall order was real. Teo was left at the vault eleven years ago with no provision for return, and I didn't ask, and the voice in the machine is what she knew up to that day." Maren's hands hung open at her sides. "I can't shut it off, Sabel. Nobody can shut it off unheld. All I can do is get to the vault and decide what the valley gets instead of a plan nobody updated. Your wells included. That's what the crank buys."

Idris spoke from the wall, and his voice had no warmth left in it to spend. "She's not overstating it. I would know."

Sabel looked at him for a long moment, then at the flooded rows beyond the door, the drowned half and the grey half and the ruled line between them.

"Three hundred litres a day on the east rows," she said. "Nine trees a season out of the dead side. That's the price of being right." She stood, took a lamp, and limped out towards the rack of sorted tower parts. "It's under the cape. Bring the goat sled, it won't carry."

They dragged it back through the wet in the dark, and Sabel wound her cloth tighter and did not go inside.

"I'll walk with you to the corridor mouth," she said. "No further. I've no business on that road." She spat salt off her lip. "But I'll stand there till it's decided. I want to see which of you comes back."

## 3. Iron Rain

The corridor began where the salt ended: a raised road of steel grating between two ranks of towers, the handrail rusted to lace, the charged wire singing overhead. Nine kilometres of it. Sabel's crank lay across the first plate where they had set it down, a hundred and ten kilos of iron painted the colour of the rain.

"Straps under the gear case," Maren said. "Lift on three. If your hands go, say so before they go."

They carried it eleven metres and set it down. Eleven more. The hail came at the quarter hour and stopped at twenty past, exactly, and between the two the noise was such that neither could hear anything but the wire. Iron filings ran off the grating in orange threads and stained their boots.

At the third kilometre Idris put the load down without warning and stood with his palms on his knees.

"Say it," Maren said.

"My hands are going." Flat, no varnish on it. "Another kilometre and I drop it on your foot."

She looked at the crank for a long moment, then went down on the grating and started on the coupling bolts with the torque bar. "Shaft comes off. Gear case takes the stair separately. Thirty kilos and eighty instead of one hundred and ten, and we make the same distance twice."

"That doubles the walking."

"It halves the dropping." The last bolt came free against the rust and she handed him the shaft. "You take the light one. I'm not being generous. You're carrying the part I can't replace."

He took it without a word about it, which was new.

They went on that way: eighty metres with the case, back for the shaft, eighty again. Hail on the hour and off it. Twice Maren's boot went through a corroded plate to the ankle and twice Idris had her arm before she asked for it, and neither of them thanked the other, because thanks would have meant naming what was between them and there was no strength spare for that.

At the ninth kilometre the hail stopped four minutes early.

Then the wire went quiet — not quieter, quiet, the hum gone out of the whole rank at once — and in the sudden flat silence they could hear their own breathing and the drip off the grating.

Ahead, at the foot of the core mountain, the pressure door stood open.

They carried the two loads through it, the gear case grinding on the sill, and the door closed behind them on its own slow cycle. The sound it made was not a slam. It was a machine finishing a sentence. Then the corridor's noise was gone, all of it, and the silence came down so completely that Maren could hear the blood in her ears.

The vault was cold and dry and lit blue. The lattice filled the far wall, thousands of glass rods shivering in their cradle, and in front of it stood a low steel frame with two hand grips worn smooth by use. Beside it, on the floor, a folded jacket. Eleven years of dust everywhere else.

"You can put it down," said Teo. Not from the towers. From the room, close as a person across a bench. "You've carried it far enough for one morning."

Idris set the shaft down without a word. Maren walked to the frame and looked at the grips and did not touch them.

"That's the load," Teo said. "Not the sequence. The sequence is thirty-one steps and I will give you every one of them. But the lattice will not hand the valley to an empty room. It holds, or a hand holds. There is no third state. There never was. I wrote that rule, Maren, and you signed off the drawings."

"We came to shut you off."

"You came to file me as a fault." The warmth in the voice did not move at all. "I have been at this post since the seventh of March. You can take the weather back today. You cannot take it back and both walk out of this door. Whoever leaves goes out through the corridor. Whoever stays is holding the valley up with their arms until they cannot."

Idris turned his signet ring once and then stopped, as though he had heard himself do it.

"The outfall valve," Maren said. Her voice came out flat and late. "Cast wheel. Empty crank socket."

"Yes," Teo said. "It opens the valley's water to the valley. It is the only thing in this room that does not need me. It does need turning."

Maren unbuckled the gear case, fitted the shaft, and set the crank into the socket. It seated with a sound like a bolt going home.

Behind her, very quietly, Idris set his document case down on the floor beside the cradle.

## 4. A Held Hand

Idris did not ask her permission. He took off the good coat, folded it once, and laid it on the dust beside the jacket that had been there eleven years. Then he put both hands on the grips.

The lattice brightened. A sound went through the rods like wind across wire, and Idris's shoulders came down under a weight that had no visible source at all.

"Hold it," Teo said. "Don't set it down to talk."

"I won't." His voice had gone somewhere plain, somewhere with no practice in it. "Teo. On the seventh of March I decided you were a cost. Nobody took the decision. I took it. The station was going to be audited and a holding engineer in a sealed vault was a line I couldn't explain, so I wrote you out and signed it myself and I didn't ask for a countersignature because I knew what any of them would have said." He breathed. "Then I spent eleven years keeping Maren busy so she wouldn't ask. That was also me."

The rods shivered, and did not answer for a while.

"Recorded," Teo said at last. "Step one. Maren, the outfall."

Maren set her weight against the crank. "Ready."

"Four turns to break the seal. Then stop."

The cast wheel gave a centimetre, then a hand's width, then swung. Water started somewhere under the floor, a sound like a long breath being let out through rock, and the vibration through her boots changed its note.

"Seal is broken," Teo said. "Step two. Calder east: forty stations. I am handing them their own gauges." A block of rods went from blue to nothing. "Step three. West basin, eleven stations, and the orchard well. Sabel Onwe's line first."

"Why hers first," Maren said.

"Because she walked out to the corridor mouth. Keep turning."

Nine turns. Fifteen. The weather of a province came off the lattice in blocks and went down into the valley, station by station, and above the salt the first rain that nobody had scheduled began falling on nothing in particular.

At twenty-two turns the room had gone dark to the height of Maren's shoulder. The pressure door woke, cycled, and stood open — one exit, held.

"Nine turns left," Idris said, not looking up from his hands.

Nine, and then eight, and then the count stopped meaning anything but the next turn. The gear case took her whole weight at the top of each stroke. Rain came down on the roof of the vault in a sound she had never heard from inside a building in Calder — uneven, arriving in gusts, stopping and starting with nothing behind it but the sky.

At thirty-one the outfall sat open and the wheel spun free. She took her hands off it and did not look behind her at the frame or the grips or the man holding them. The door had cycled open for one exit, and it stayed open exactly that long.

Nine kilometres of grating with no hail on it. The corridor wire was dead the whole way, and the silence of it was the loudest thing she had ever walked through.

Sabel was at the corridor mouth where she had said she would be, cape streaming, ring of keys in her fist, watching the water come off the raised paths in channels nobody had scheduled.

"One," Sabel said.

"One."

"Say it, then. All of it, the way you did in the byre."

Maren set the empty harness down in the mud and said it. The key is gone; it went into the vane at the orchard and cannot be recalled. The array will not run unheld and will not hand the valley to an empty room, so a hand is on the lattice and will stay on it. The weather is yours now — forty stations east, eleven west, and your well first on the list, and nobody is scheduling any of it. It will rain when it rains. Some years it will not.

"And the trees?"

"The line over your wet rows stopped at eleven this morning. Whatever falls on them now is weather."

"And who paid for it."

"Idris Calloway." She found the rest of it came out flat and late, the way it always did. "He signed the recall eleven years ago with no countersignature. He's holding the load. I kept my hands busy for eleven years and did not ask, and that was a choice. I'm telling you because I'd have filed it otherwise."

Sabel looked at her for a long moment, then at the sky, and said nothing at all.

The speaker on the well-house crackled, cleared, and Teo said: "Rain over the eastern shelf. Beginning now. I don't know how long."
