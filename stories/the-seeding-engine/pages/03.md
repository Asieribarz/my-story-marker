They were at the seventh turn when the voice came down the scree, off the station aerials behind and above them, unhurried as a duty log.

"The sluices opened at ten past," Teodora said. "Outfall is four cubic metres a second and rising to eleven. The pass floods to the twelfth turn. You have forty minutes of dry footing."

Idris stopped with one hand against the rock. He turned the signet ring once around his finger. "There was a shutdown window," he said, "and it was allowed to close."

"Recorded. Nobody answered."

Maren was already reading the trough. The dark line had become water, brown and ropey, shouldering along the channel with a sound like a crowd arriving. She counted the switchbacks below her against the chart she did not need to take out of her pocket.

"Forty minutes," she said. "Five turns. We walk them in twenty. Move."

They moved. Idris's city boots skated the scree and she took his elbow twice without slowing, and then she let go and took the last five turns at a run, and he came after her breathing badly. At the ninth turn the water lipped the trough wall and laid a sheet across the track behind them, thin and bright. At the eleventh it was over their boots. At the twelfth it was at the knee and hard, and then the pass was gone behind them and they were on the salt, soaked to the thigh, with eighteen kilometres of white in front of them and nothing at all at their backs.

Maren turned around to look.

The station stood on the ridge lip for a while yet. Then the spillway took the gallery under it, and the concrete leaned, and it went down the scree in one piece, slowly, the way a thing goes that has been undermined rather than struck. The aerials went with it. The condenser vane was the last to turn over, and it was still turning when the water closed.

The voice off the ridge stopped in the middle of a number.

"That was the only relay," Maren said.

"Yes," Idris said.

Neither of them said the rest of it. Nobody would hear anything from Calder now until one of them walked out of it, and the array had four days to decide whether either of them would.

Out on the white, the nearest tower lamp swivelled, found them, and held.
