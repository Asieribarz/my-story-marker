The basin took them for two days, and it took them on the array's schedule.

Fog came at first light and stayed until the towers found them, a white column that cut visibility to nine metres and made the hexagonal plates clack invisibly underfoot. When it lifted at noon it lifted all at once, and the glare came down like a lid. Maren counted towers. Eleven to the middle distance, six beyond it, each a hundred metres of lattice with its lamp turning slowly to keep them in view.

"Hail at four," she said on the second afternoon, watching the head of tower nine go dark. "Get under the coat."

It fell for eleven minutes, stones the size of knuckles, and stopped on a clean edge. Idris came out from under the storm shell laughing the way he laughed when he was frightened, and turning the signet ring. "A demonstration, presumably. The point having been made, one assumes we're allowed to continue."

"It's not making a point. It's running a plan." She looked at the ruin of his city boots. "Three litres left. Move."

The eastern shelf showed as a green line at the end of the second day, and the green resolved into pear and almond, and the trees resolved into two halves divided by a mark as sharp as a ruled line — half standing in shallow water, half grey and dead a metre from it. Rain fell on the hour in a straight column out of nothing worth calling a cloud, and stopped.

The well-keeper was waiting on the raised path with a ring of keys in her fist and goats behind her.

"You'll be the engineers," Sabel Onwe said. "The byre's dry and there's a well I'll let you drink from. Before that." She nodded at the wet half of the orchard. "That line falls every third day. Eight hundred trees live on it. What are you two going to do to it?"

Idris began: "There are arrangements that would need—"

"Him I'm not asking." She had not moved her eyes off Maren. "You've a crank on your harness. You'll want mine too, I expect. So say it plain, and you sleep under a roof."

Maren set her hands on the harness. "We're going to shut the array down. After that the valley gets whatever weather it gets."

Sabel nodded slowly, as though a bill had come in at the figure she'd expected.

"Right," she said. "That's what it told me. Three days ago, out of the well-house speaker. Said two engineers were coming." She turned for the byre. "It gave your names. Yours first."
