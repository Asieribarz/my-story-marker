Three months into Madrid, Marco's relationship to the city fundamentally shifts. He stops planning to leave. He stops calculating return dates. He stops thinking of his illegal status as temporary. Instead, he begins to notice things: the way the light falls differently on different streets, the sound the metro makes at different stations, the café where the owner knows him by face if not by name. He is becoming less foreign.

Work continues. The construction site changes locations every few weeks, and with each site comes different people, different challenges, the same fundamental precarity. But Marco's reputation grows. Other foremen ask for him specifically. Javier tells him he has become one of the best supervisors on the underground circuit, which is to say: reliable, competent, and willing to take on impossible tasks.

One evening, Elena calls. She has not heard from him since he moved out, and her voice carries the tone of someone delivering inevitable bad news.

"My mother is arriving tomorrow," she says. "From Morocco. She wants to stay a month. I know this is strange, but... would you want to help me prepare? Come cook dinner? I could use a friend."

Marco goes. Elena's apartment looks exactly the same—the maps, the objects, the mirror—and in that sameness, Marco recognizes what he has been avoiding: Elena was never judging him. She was simply witnessing him. They cook a tagine, which Elena learned to make from her mother when she was younger. They drink wine. For the first time in months, Marco talks about his mother, about the lie, about the weight of maintaining a false version of himself across the distance of an ocean.

"She doesn't know?" Elena asks.

"She cannot know. She has told everyone in Lima that I am successful here. If I admit it's a lie, I destroy her."

"You think she doesn't already know something is wrong?"

"Maybe. But knowing and being told are different things. As long as I don't tell her, there is still hope."

Elena refills his wine. "Hope for what?"

Marco doesn't answer because he realizes he doesn't know. Hope that he will become an architect again? Hope that the firm will reopen? Hope that his mother will forgive him? All of these seem equally impossible.

The next morning, Elena's mother arrives, and Marco meets her: a woman of seventy with Elena's directness and twice her wariness. She looks at Marco and says simply, "You are in trouble."

"No," Marco says.

"Yes," she says. "I have lived through exile three times. I know the face of someone displaced. You are in trouble."

That afternoon, Elena's mother—Fatima, the same Fatima who rents rooms to undeclared workers—introduces Marco to her network. There are more of them than he realized: a nurse from Nigeria, a software engineer from Pakistan, a teacher from Moldova, all working jobs that have nothing to do with their training, all maintaining lies to their families, all settling into Madrid in the gap between official invisibility and actual presence.

They meet in Fatima's kitchen, in the apartment above the rooms she rents. They eat good food and speak Spanish and Arabic and Portuguese. No one asks questions about legal status. No one pretends to be someone else. And for the first time since arriving in Madrid, Marco experiences a strange, fragile peace: he is not alone in his invisibility.

Elena's mother says to him: "This is not the life you planned."

"No," Marco says.

"But it is a life. If you can stay alive, if you can work, if you can eat—this is something. My daughter thinks you are important. I see why. You do not break easily."

That week, one of the construction supervisors asks Marco if he wants to take on a project-lead role. Not a legal position—still undeclared, still illegal—but visible leadership, better pay, real responsibility. Marco takes it.

He is building something. Not the something he came to Madrid to build, but something. And in that act of building, he begins, for the first time, to imagine a future that doesn't require him to stop lying to his family. A future where the lie becomes permanent, and permanent things can become true.
