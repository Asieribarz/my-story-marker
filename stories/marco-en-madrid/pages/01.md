Madrid's Barajas Airport rises around Marco like a cathedral of glass and steel. He has traveled thirty hours—Lima to Miami to Madrid—and the exhaustion sits behind his eyes like a weight he cannot quite set down. But beneath the fatigue is something lighter: possibility. His phone in one hand, luggage in the other, and in his jacket pocket, a signed contract that says *Arquitectura Herrera y Asociados*, starting salary, benefits, relocation stipend already received. He touches the pocket twice as he walks toward the arrivals hall, a gesture somewhere between prayer and confirmation.

Elena is waiting beyond customs, holding a handwritten sign that reads *BIENVENIDO* in letters so large they seem to announce not just Marco but the entire principle of his arrival. She is the friend of a friend, someone who agreed three weeks ago to collect him from the airport and let him stay "a night or two" before he found his own place. She is forty-something, with the practical stance of a woman who has absorbed multiple geographies into her spine. When Marco sees her, he almost weeps.

"Dios mío, you look like you've been in that plane for a month," Elena says, embracing him. "Come on, my car is close."

In the taxi—Elena drives an old Seat with the temperament of a living thing—they navigate the curved ramps of the airport parking structure. Madrid spreads beyond the windows: low buildings, unfamiliar trees, the light at a different angle than he remembers from the photos he studied. Elena speaks in a rapid, unsentimental Spanish, pointing out landmarks. "That's where you'll see the worst traffic. That café has excellent coffee. The metro is there—you'll use it constantly."

Marco nods, trying to absorb the city while also holding the contract in his mind. The contract is real. His finger still remembers the texture of the signature line.

"How is your visa situation?" Elena asks, merging into traffic.

"All arranged. Sixty days to find work or I need to apply for an extension." Marco taps the passport in his other jacket pocket. "But I have the job."

"Already?"

"Signed contract. Herrera y Asociados. Architecture firm. Good one, apparently."

Elena glances at him, and something crosses her face—a flicker of something between interest and skepticism—but it passes too quickly to name. "That's fantastic," she says. "That never happens. Usually people come here and hunt for months."

"I know. I was lucky." Marco feels the need to explain, though Elena has not questioned him. "I worked with one of their old partners in Lima. He remembered me. Made the introduction."

"Then you're set."

They drive in silence for a while, and Marco watches Madrid accumulate around him: traffic, storefronts, people on motorcycles. The city looks simultaneously exactly like every city and completely foreign. He presses his forehead against the cool window, listening to Elena's commentary about neighborhoods and rent prices and where the best *pan de chocolate* is sold.

By the time they reach Elena's apartment—small, organized, hung with objects from countries he cannot name—the sun is setting. Elena shows him the couch where he will sleep. "You can stay as long as you need," she says. "No rush."

"Just a few weeks," Marco says. "Until I find a place near the office."

That night, alone on Elena's couch, Marco texts his mother: *Llegué bien. La ciudad es hermosa. Mañana empiezo en la oficina.* I arrived safely. The city is beautiful. Tomorrow I start at the office. She responds with a chain of prayer emojis and exclamation points.

He touches the contract one more time before sleep takes him.

Tomorrow, his life begins.
