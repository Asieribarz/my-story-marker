The decision happens in the shower. Marco stands under lukewarm water—Elena's apartment's pressure is weak—and realizes he has three choices: tell his mother the truth immediately, wait a few days and tell her the truth with a grace period, or lie. He turns off the water and stands dripping, fully aware that he is at a threshold. He closes his eyes and crosses it.

His mother answers the video call from her kitchen in Lima. It is morning there. She is making coffee, moving between the stove and the table with the efficiency of someone who has done this ten thousand times. She sees his face on the screen and her own face transforms—pride, relief, hunger to be near him. "Mi amor, how is it? How is the job? How is Madrid?"

"Perfect," Marco says. The word leaves his mouth and he realizes he has committed to a shape. "Everything is perfect. The office is beautiful. The people are smart. The project I'm on is interesting—a residential complex in the north of the city."

None of this is true, but he can see his mother believing it, and in that belief, he finds something solid to stand on. She tells him about the neighborhood in Lima, about his younger sister starting a new job, about the priest's sermon last Sunday. They talk for twenty-three minutes. When he ends the call, he feels both dead and alive.

Elena does not ask him what he told his mother. She simply leaves a job listing on the kitchen table: *Se busca ayudantes de construcción. Sin experiencia necesaria. Pago diario.* Construction workers wanted. No experience necessary. Daily pay.

The construction site is in a working-class neighborhood east of the city center. Marco arrives at five in the morning when the sky is still purple. A man is already there with a clipboard, assigning men—most of them foreign, most of them moving slowly with the resignation of people who have done this many times—to different tasks. The man looks at Marco once and gestures to a pile of bricks.

"Can you carry?" the man asks.

"Yes," Marco says.

"Then carry."

He carries bricks. His hands, which have never done anything rougher than hold a pencil, begin to blister immediately. His back, which has hunched over design tables for fifteen years, screams. The other workers ignore him mostly, except for one man—Colombian, maybe forty, with the kind of physical presence that comes from a decade of labor. This man's name is Javier.

"First day?" Javier asks during the mid-morning break.

"Yes."

"You're going to be destroyed," Javier says, not unkindly. "Your hands are too soft. In three days, if you last, they will look like mine." He holds up his hands. The palms are calloused, stained with concrete, marked with old scars.

"How long have you been here?" Marco asks.

"Doing this specific site? Three weeks. Doing construction? Thirteen years. In Madrid? Ten." Javier takes a drink of water from a plastic bottle. "And I'm here illegally, which means every day I can be reported. You legal?"

Marco almost lies, but something in Javier's directness stops him. "No," he says. "Tourist visa."

"So you're fucked like me." Javier nods, as if this confirms something. "The only difference is I've had time to get used to it. Come on. They want us back."

That afternoon, carrying bricks with his hands on fire and his back in agony, Marco makes a second decision: he tells his company—if one can call it that—that he has architecture experience. The foreman nods. "Then you supervise the eastern corner," he says. "Make sure those walls are plumb. We pay five euros more per hour for supervisors."

Marco has never supervised construction before, but he has read about it, studied it, imagined it. He walks the eastern corner, measures the walls with his eye, and catches a mistake that would have been costly. He shows the worker how to adjust. The worker does it, and the wall becomes true.

At day's end, Javier is impressed. "You actually know what you're doing," he says.

"I'm an architect," Marco says. It is the first true thing he has said all day. "But the firm I was hired for closed."

"So you're here until they hire you somewhere else."

"Something like that," Marco says, which is true but incomplete. What he means is: I am here until the lie becomes so solid that the truth cannot touch it.

The work is brutal. The lie is working. These are the two facts Marco holds as he rides the metro back to Elena's apartment, every muscle burning, ten euros richer, his future entirely uncertain.
