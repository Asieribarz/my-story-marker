Two months into undeclared work, Marco makes a decision that surprises him: he will attempt to become legal. Not because he believes it will work, but because not attempting it feels like accepting defeat without testing it. He researches Spanish immigration law at an internet café, takes notes, and makes an appointment at the *Oficina de Extranjería*—the immigration office where foreign status is determined.

The office is in a modern building, functional and beige. The waiting area has plastic chairs bolted to the floor and a digital display showing whose turn it is. There is a smell of cleaning products and resignation. Marco arrives at 7 AM to get an appointment for later that day. By 8 AM, there are already fifty people waiting.

The appointment comes at 3 PM. A man named Miguel Ferrero sits behind a desk, reviewing Marco's documents with the expression of someone processing information that has nothing to do with him. Miguel is in his late fifties, wearing a shirt in a neutral color, and he speaks in the passive voice, as if decisions are made by the universe rather than by him.

"You arrived on a tourist visa sixty days ago," Miguel says, reading from a file.

"Yes. I came because I had a job offer."

"The job offer is no longer valid, I see."

"The firm closed."

"That is unfortunate." Miguel does not look up. "You have several options. You can return to Peru. You can apply for a work visa, but for that you need an employer to sponsor you. Do you have an employer?"

"I have worked construction, but it is undeclared."

"Then it is not employment according to Spanish law." Miguel adjusts his glasses. "Undeclared work is illegal both for you and for the employer. I cannot regularize employment that is not legal."

"So what can I do?"

"You can return to Peru. You can attempt to find a legal employer, which requires a work permit, which requires legal employment. This is a structure." Miguel says it as if he is describing the laws of physics. "You are currently in Spain illegally. Your tourist visa expired at day sixty. Every day beyond that is a violation."

"Can I apply for any kind of extension? Any path to stay?"

Miguel closes the file. "There are paths for asylum, for family reunification, for specific work visas in shortage fields. Architecture is not in shortage. You have no family in Spain. You are not fleeing persecution. The paths that exist do not apply to you."

"What if I study? Could I be a student?"

"You would need proof of income to support yourself as a student, university enrollment, and a valid visa. If you do not have the income, you cannot study. If you cannot study, you cannot maintain a student visa. Again, a structure."

Marco sits with this for a moment. The structure Miguel describes is airtight. It is not cruel, exactly. It is simply indifferent. It does not hate Marco. It does not reject him personally. It simply does not have a category for him.

"So I have no legal options."

"You have options," Miguel says. "None of them involve you remaining in Madrid legally. That is what the data shows."

When the appointment ends, Marco is given a printed notice stating that he must leave Spain within thirty days or face deportation proceedings. The notice is so formal, so official, that it carries the weight of inevitability. He leaves the office feeling as if the city has officially, bureaucratically, rejected him.

That evening, he does not call his mother. Instead, he walks through Madrid alone. He walks through neighborhoods he does not know, past people living lives that have nothing to do with him. He realizes, somewhere on a street in a neighborhood he cannot name, that the version of himself that believed in legal solutions is dead. The architect who came to Spain to build a career has been deported—not from the country, but from possibility.

When he returns to the basement room, Javier is sitting on the front steps smoking a cigarette.

"You look destroyed," Javier says.

"I went to immigration."

"Ah." Javier nods as if this confirms something sad he has long suspected. "They told you there is no path."

"They told me the structure does not have a place for me."

"There is no structure," Javier says. "There is only the way things actually work, and then there is what the office says. We live in the gap between them." He flicks his cigarette into the street. "You were always going to end up here. You just took longer to realize it."

That night, Marco updates his mother with another lie: the firm is going through a restructuring, but his position is stable. He is saving money. All is well. She believes him because she needs to. He believes his lie because the alternative is to admit that the man she raised, the one she invested in, is invisible to everyone but her, and even his invisibility is slowly erasing her pride in him.

He has stopped expecting the system to save him. The only question left is: can he save himself?
