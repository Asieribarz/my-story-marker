The address on the contract is in a business district north of the city center. Marco has rehearsed the metro route twice the night before, written down the stop name, planned to arrive thirty minutes early. He wears the pressed shirt and the Italian shoes, though Elena suggested—gently, with the tone of someone dispensing hard wisdom—that he dress more modestly. "You'll stand out," she said. Marco wore the shirt anyway. He wants to arrive looking like what he is: someone chosen for this job because he earned it.

The building is glass and steel like the airport, fifteen stories, the lobby efficiently beautiful. On the directory, *Arquitectura Herrera y Asociados* is listed on the eighth floor. Marco's stomach feels tight as the elevator rises.

The office door on the eighth floor is locked.

Not closed. Locked. Through the frosted glass, he can see emptiness: chairs overturned, no lights, no movement. Marco tries the handle twice, presses the buzzer. Nothing. He checks the directory again. Eighth floor. Arquitectura Herrera. But the door does not open. A security guard passes in the hallway. "Can I help you?" the guard asks.

"I'm looking for Arquitectura Herrera," Marco says. "Eighth floor."

The guard's face becomes sympathetic, which is a worse response than indifference would have been. "They closed two weeks ago. Something happened with the partner. I'd check the news."

Marco's legs feel strange. He sits on a bench in the lobby and pulls out his phone. Arquitectura Herrera y Asociados. News. Three articles from two weeks ago, all in Spanish, all saying essentially the same thing: the firm has dissolved, one partner dead—a heart attack—the other unable to continue the business alone. The office is closed. All contracts cancelled pending legal review.

His contract, signed four weeks ago, is a document about a company that no longer exists.

He calls the number on the contract—the contact number for his supposed supervisor—and a woman answers after six rings. "I'm sorry, who is this?" she says when he introduces himself. "I'm not with Herrera anymore. I'm not sure how you got this number." She sounds harried, guilty. "I'm sorry. I can't help you with that. You need to contact the legal office."

Marco does contact the legal office. They tell him there is nothing they can do. All pending contracts are void. The company is in dissolution. He should seek other employment. This is said by an automated system followed by a disconnection.

He sits in that building lobby for forty minutes, watching people move through the space with purpose, with destinations. They are all going to jobs that exist. His job is hypothetical. His contract is void. The thing that made him brave—the thing that separated him from Elena's couch and poverty and shame—is paper.

When he returns to Elena's apartment that evening, she is making dinner. She takes one look at his face and does not ask what happened. She simply sets out another plate.

"Tell me," she says.

He tells her. She listens, and does not say *I told you so*, though he can feel the thought beneath her silence.

"What now?" Marco asks.

Elena stirs the pot. "Now we figure it out. You're not the first person this has happened to."

But Marco knows he is alone in this. His visa is temporary. His money will not last forever. And his family in Lima believes he has already won.
