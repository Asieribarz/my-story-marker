# World rules

r1. **Visa restrictions bind choices.** Marco holds a tourist visa with limited work authorization. He cannot legally work without a work permit, but obtaining one requires employer sponsorship—creating a catch-22 that forces illegal or undeclared work.
r2. **Professional credentials don't transfer.** His Peruvian architecture degree is not automatically recognized in Spain. Revalidation requires bureaucratic processes that cost time and money he doesn't have. His expertise becomes worthless on paper.
r3. **Madrid is a city of invisible hierarchies.** Class, origin, and accent mark people instantly. Marco's Peruvian Spanish identifies him as foreign, which closes certain doors while opening others (service work, construction, ethnic enclaves).
r4. **Money runs out faster than hope.** Marco arrived with savings meant for the first three months. Each month without legal income erodes both resources and credibility. There is a hard bottom—eviction, return, or crisis.
r5. **Family expectations are a form of debt.** Marco's family in Lima believes in his success story. Admitting failure is not just personal shame; it is a betrayal of their investment and faith. The lie becomes harder to sustain than the struggle.
r6. **Displacement creates temporary communities.** Other migrants, expats, and displaced people in Madrid form networks of shared vulnerability. These communities operate by different rules—mutual aid, shared housing, unspoken agreements about not asking too many questions.
r7. **Work without meaning feels like erasure.** Taking any job to survive means entering work that is not his. Each day in construction or kitchen work is a day not building, not designing—a day the architect in him dies a little.
r8. **True rest requires acceptance.** Peace in Madrid is not possible while clinging to the identity of "the architect who was supposed to arrive." Acceptance is not defeat; it is the price of being alive.
