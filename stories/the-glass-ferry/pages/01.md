The light came through the harbor windows and fell across Kael's face, which was how Paine had arranged the room, and Kael had sat in worse chairs in worse weather and did not shift.

"Thirty years," Merchant Paine said. He turned a ring on his finger without looking at it. "Thirty years and not one passenger lost. Do you know what that is worth, ferryman?"

"It's worth the next crossing," Kael said.

Paine smiled. It was a broad, warm-shaped thing that did not warm anything. He drew a purse from the drawer and set it on the desk between them, and the sound it made was not the sound of a small purse.

"One passenger. Tonight, on the tide. Lean, dark clothes, carries a bundle of papers and nothing else. Answers to Vex, though I doubt they will answer to anything." He pushed the purse an inch forward. "Four times your fare. The papers belong to me. So does the person carrying them."

Kael looked at the money and did not touch it. Beyond the glass, the harbor moved—cranes, carts, the ordinary commerce of a shore where the law still reached. Two miles west of it the mist began, and out there the only law was what a man agreed to.

"You want them carried across," he said.

"I want them carried out." Paine folded his hands. "Into the Strait. And I want the far shore to go on being what it always is from the water—invisible. No witness on either bank. No boat but yours. You would not even have to be quick about it."

The room absorbed the words the way it absorbed everything, and left them sitting there.

"And the passenger is told nothing," Kael said.

"The passenger is told the fare and the weather. That is the compact, ferryman. It has always been the compact." Paine's smile did not move. "Take the purse and it is agreed. Leave it, and I find someone who will—and then I remember, for the rest of my life, that you refused me."

There is no other ferryman on the Strait. They both knew it, and only one of them said nothing about it.

Kael took the purse. It was heavier than money ought to be.

He went down the stairs into the harbor air with his hand around it, and every step he took toward the water was a step he had already sold.
