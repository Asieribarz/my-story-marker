## 1. The Passenger

The light came through the harbor windows and fell across Kael's face, which was how Paine had arranged the room, and Kael had sat in worse chairs in worse weather and did not shift.

"Thirty years," Merchant Paine said. He turned a ring on his finger without looking at it. "Thirty years and not one passenger lost. Do you know what that is worth, ferryman?"

"It's worth the next crossing," Kael said.

Paine smiled. It was a broad, warm-shaped thing that did not warm anything. He drew a purse from the drawer and set it on the desk between them, and the sound it made was not the sound of a small purse.

"One passenger. Tonight, on the tide. Lean, dark clothes, carries a bundle of papers and nothing else. Answers to Vex, though I doubt they will answer to anything." He pushed the purse an inch forward. "Four times your fare. The papers belong to me. So does the person carrying them."

Kael looked at the money and did not touch it. Beyond the glass, the harbor moved—cranes, carts, the ordinary commerce of a shore where the law still reached. Two miles west of it the mist began, and out there the only law was what a man agreed to.

"You want them carried across," he said.

"I want them carried out." Paine folded his hands. "Into the Strait. And I want the far shore to go on being what it always is from the water—invisible. No witness on either bank. No boat but yours. You would not even have to be quick about it."

The room absorbed the words the way it absorbed everything, and left them sitting there.

"And the passenger is told nothing," Kael said.

"The passenger is told the fare and the weather. That is the compact, ferryman. It has always been the compact." Paine's smile did not move. "Take the purse and it is agreed. Leave it, and I find someone who will—and then I remember, for the rest of my life, that you refused me."

There is no other ferryman on the Strait. They both knew it, and only one of them said nothing about it.

Kael took the purse. It was heavier than money ought to be.

He went down the stairs into the harbor air with his hand around it, and every step he took toward the water was a step he had already sold.

Orin had the lines coiled and the sail ready before Kael reached the slip, and he grinned the way he always did when he had finished a job early. "Tide's slack for another hour, isn't it? Or is it turning? It felt like turning when I was down on the rocks. Should I reef the sail before we go out, or after? You always say after, but the wind—"

"After," Kael said, and put the purse in the cabin locker without looking at it.

The passenger was already aboard, sitting at the foot of the mast with their back against it and their knees drawn up, a small bundle of documents pressed under one arm. Dark clothes. Nothing about them worth remembering. They had come down the quay without a sound and boarded without asking permission, which was the mark of someone who had learned that asking invites refusal.

"Vex," they said. Nothing more.

Kael took the tiller. The Glass Ferry came off the dock the way she always came off it, sweet and unhurried, and then the harbor mouth slid past and the mist closed behind them like a door, and that was that. No stopping now. No turning. The near shore was gone as though it had never been built.

For the first half hour Vex did not move at all. It was Orin who noticed, because Orin was young and looked at people instead of at water.

"You're wet," he said. "Sir—ma'am—you're sitting in water. No, that's not—Kael. Kael, that's not water."

Vex's hand came up to cover it, too late and too slow. Under the dark coat the dark cloth had gone darker still, and where they had been sitting the deck held a slick black pool that the boat's roll was already stretching into a finger, crawling toward the scupper.

"It's bound," Vex said. Their voice did not rise. "It will hold to the far shore. There are people at the sanctuary who can close it. Three hours. I can do three hours."

"And if I turned back?" Kael heard himself ask.

Vex looked at him then, steady as stone, and answered without any weight on it at all.

"Then you'd be carrying a corpse into the harbor, and you'd have to explain it."

Kael's hand stayed on the tiller. Behind him, in the locker, the purse of a man who had paid him four times over to never reach the far shore shifted with the swell and knocked once against the wood.

Three aboard, and none of them speaking. Vex sat where they had sat since the harbor mouth, at the foot of the mast, the bundle of documents dry under one arm and the deck beneath them no longer dry at all. Orin was forward, a grey outline at the bow with his back to the cabin, where he had stayed since he coiled the last of the lines before the mist took the shore. The water had gone loose and quick under the hull. Kael felt it come through the tiller the way he felt weather in his knuckles.

"You're thinking about turning," Vex said.

Kael did not answer. The rule of the Strait answered for him: there was no turning, not once the current had you. But a man could think it, and a man who had taken four times his fare could think it the way a prayer is thought.

"You can't," Vex went on, quiet, unhurried, choosing each word as though it cost them something. "Not the water — I know the water. I mean the shore. Paine has men on that quay who have been there since dark. If this boat comes back with me breathing, they kill me on the sand. If it comes back with me dead, they pay you and forget you. Either way the near shore is where I end."

"And the far one?"

"The far one is a chance. It's the only chance there is." They shifted, and the pain of it showed for a half second before the stillness closed again. "Three hours. I said three hours and I meant it. Forward is the only direction that has me alive at the end of it."

The purse knocked once more in the locker, and Kael heard it as what it was — a price, and a small one, set against thirty years.

"Then forward," he said.

He said it without flourish, the way he said a crossing was safe, and in saying it he understood exactly what it would cost him and agreed to the cost. He set the tiller for the deep channel, where the rocks were, where a careful man went when he intended to arrive.

## 2. The Cost

The water changed an hour out.

Kael felt it first through the tiller, the way a man feels a fever in another man's hand. The long easy swells that had carried them out of the harbor mouth broke apart into something short and crossed and angry, and the Glass Ferry began to shudder along her whole length, timber speaking to timber from prow to stern.

"Orin. Sheet in. Half a hand, no more."

"Half a hand." The boy was already moving, and for once he did not ask why. He hauled, snubbed the line, and looked back for the nod.

Out of the mist to starboard came a sound like cloth tearing: water breaking white over the rocks at the Strait's heart. Kael did not look at it. He had never once needed to look at it. He leaned on the tiller and put the bow a little across the run of the current, feeling for the seam of deep water that ran between the rocks and the shoal, the channel he had found by feel when he was younger than Orin and had been finding ever since.

The ferry heeled. Green water came over the rail and swept the deck ankle-deep, and Orin lost his footing on the wet oak and went down hard against the cabin.

A hand caught him by the collar before the next roll could take him further. Vex. Kneeling, braced, holding the bundle of documents clear under one arm and the boy with the other, face as still as a shut door.

"The rope," Vex said. "Behind you. Take a turn round your waist."

Orin took it. He was shaking, and he did it anyway, and then he reached back and hauled on the sheet with Vex's weight added to his own, the two of them working the line together while the deck tilted and the mist poured past.

"Hold her there," Kael said. "Both of you. Hold her there."

They held. The Glass Ferry went through the crossed water the way she had gone through it for fifty years, and came out the other side into swells long and slow and almost gentle, and the sound of the rocks fell away behind them.

Nobody spoke for a while. Orin was still holding the rope; Vex was still holding Orin. Somewhere ahead, close now, the mist carried the smell of sand.

It was Orin who heard it first, and he heard it because he was lying flat on the deck with his ear a handspan from the boards. He said Kael's name twice before he got the rest out. "There's water. Under me. There's water where there shouldn't be water, it's not spray, it's coming up, Kael, it's coming up through the—"

"Move."

Kael gave the tiller to Vex without asking whether they knew what to do with it. "Hold her as she lies. Don't correct. Whatever she does, don't correct." Then he was down on his knees at the cabin step with his hand flat against the oak, feeling the way he felt the water, and his face did not change at all.

The rocks had taken her along the turn of the bilge, low and long, where a man could not see it and could not reach it. He did not need to see it. He knew every beam and plank of her, and one of them had opened.

"Can you stop it," Vex said. Not a question, the way they said it.

"No."

He stood. The mist was as it had always been, close and white and giving nothing away, and somewhere behind them was the harbor he had left at the turn of the tide, and somewhere ahead was sand he could smell but not see. He could still pilot her. That was the cruelty of it. She would swim another hour, maybe more, and he was ferryman enough to put her anywhere he chose inside that hour. Back was two hours against the current and would kill her halfway, and there were men waiting on that quay since dark.

Forward would kill her too. Forward would only kill her at the right end.

Fifty years she had crossed this water and never once gone down. He had inherited her the way other men inherit a name.

"Orin," he said. "Get the bailing bucket. Not to save her. To buy her time."

"Save her how? Kael—"

"She isn't being saved." He took the tiller back out of Vex's hands, gently, and felt her answer, heavier already, slower coming round. "She's being finished."

He put the prow at the far shore and held it there.

The water came up through the boards faster than Orin could throw it over. Kael heard the change in the boat under him the way another man hears a friend's breathing turn shallow — the roll gone heavy, the rise off each swell a little later each time, as though the Glass Ferry had grown tired of asking.

"Keep bailing," he said. "Not to save her. To keep her nose up another hundred yards."

Orin bailed. He did not ask the question on his face, and Kael thought that might be the first thing the boy had learned that could not be taught.

The mist gave up the far shore all at once, the way it always did: no gradual thinning, no grey suggestion of land, only rock and steep beach and a black line of hills standing suddenly close enough to touch. Kael felt the bottom come up to meet him before he saw it, that shallowing under the hull he had known for thirty years, and he laid the tiller over and let the last of the swell carry her in.

The prow struck sand. The whole boat shuddered once, gently, like a man setting down a weight he had carried a long way.

"Go," Kael said.

Vex stood a moment in the bow with the bundle of documents held against the bound wound, and looked at him. "Paine paid you," they said. It was not a question, and there was no anger in it. They had worked it out somewhere in the crossed water, perhaps earlier.

"He did," Kael said.

"And you brought me anyway."

"I did."

Vex nodded once, precise, wasting nothing, and went over the rail into the shallows. The water took them to the knee. They climbed the rocky beach without hurrying, found the narrow path, and the mist closed around their shoulders and then their head, and then there was only the path and the wet green growing over it, and no one on either bank to say they had ever been there at all.

Kael did not move. Behind him Orin set the bucket down without being told. The Glass Ferry settled by the stern, slow and unprotesting, the water climbing the cabin door, the sill, the small square window that smelled of oil and wood and salt; and then it climbed over the roof and was level and still, and the boat that had carried him across the Strait for thirty years had made its last crossing.
