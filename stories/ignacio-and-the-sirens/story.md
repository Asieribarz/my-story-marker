# The Hair-Rope

*An adventure in 10 pages.*


## 1. The Hair-Rope

The rope-walk behind the fish market sold hair by weight and asked no questions, which was the only reason Ignacio Robles had used it for eleven years.

"Nine pounds," the woman said. "There is no more in Cadiz. There is no more in Sanlucar either, before you walk there."

"Nine pounds is a hundred and twenty feet."

"Then you have a hundred and twenty feet."

He paid her and carried the coil out into the white light off the harbour wall, where Pau was sitting on the bollard with his legs swinging, and Don Fausto Delgado was standing in the shade of the bond office with his hat on, sixty years old and already looking at the water as if it had insulted him.

"Captain." Fausto had the ledger under his arm, the one with the brass lock. "Before you put that aboard."

He unfolded the bond on the top of a fish crate and held it flat with two fingers against the wind. Ignacio had read it twice already. He read the third clause again anyway, because Fausto had stopped there with his thumb.

*A living specimen, delivered whole and breathing to a place of the financier's choosing, before witnesses of the financier's choosing.*

"Living," Ignacio said.

"Living. A dead one is a fish, and I have seen four fish in twenty years and paid for two of them." Fausto folded the paper along its old creases. "You have had three commissions from three men. Each of them you came back to with a story. I am not buying a story. I have been laughed at once and it was enough."

"Understood."

"I do not think it is. So." He put the ledger back under his arm. "I am sailing with you."

Pau's legs stopped swinging.

"There is no cabin," Ignacio said.

"I shall have yours, and you shall have the deck, and neither of us will enjoy it." Fausto was already walking toward the slip, a dry man going carefully over wet stone. "Sixty-one days, captain. After that the bond is forfeit and the ship is mine, and I shall sell her to somebody who fishes."

Three days out, with the land gone and the ship going along easy on the starboard tack, Brites Olaya came up to the weather rail and stood there long enough that Ignacio knew what was coming.

"Say it to the boy," she said.

"He has heard it."

"He has heard the quay version. Nine men have told him the quay version and no two of them agreed, which is the whole trouble with this ship." She did not look at him. "Say it once, properly, and then I will stop asking."

Pau was at the foot of the mainmast pretending to serve a rope's end. He had given up pretending by the second sentence.

"I was twenty," Ignacio said. "I was on a shoal boat off the Restinga and the boat went over in nothing at all, in flat water, the way they do. I could not swim then and I cannot swim now."

"Go on."

"I was in the water a night and a day. I did not tread it. I did not float. Something held me up under both arms from behind, the whole time, and it breathed, and when the Sanlucar boat came over the edge of the shoal in the morning it let go of me and I saw an arm go down past my leg with a tear across the forearm, here, from the wrist to the elbow, and then there was nothing to see and the bottom was two fathoms down and bright as a plate."

"And the Sanlucar boat," Brites said.

"Four men in it."

"And what did the four men see?"

"One saw a woman. One saw a seal. Two saw me shouting at nothing in flat water, and one of those two is a priest now in Jerez." He turned the glass over under his shirt without knowing he had. "Rule four. Nobody has ever beaten it. That is why I need her aboard and not in anybody's memory."

Pau said, "Captain, what day do we raise the shoal?"

"The nineteenth."

"That is a night and a day from now."

"It is."

"Captain." The boy's voice had gone careful. "The nineteenth is eleven years. To the day."

Brites looked at Ignacio for a long moment, and then she went below without saying anything at all, which from her was the loudest thing available.

They came onto the Restinga an hour after dawn on the nineteenth, and the sea changed colour under the bow the way it always does at the edge: green, then a green so pale it is almost no colour, and then the bottom, two fathoms down and lying there in the light like something in a shop window. Sand. Lanes of weed going away west. The black ribs of a ship nobody had ever named.

Ignacio had the lead going and did not need it.

Pau was at the crosstrees because he was light and because he had the best ears aboard, and at twenty minutes past the hour he stopped calling the water and went quiet, and then he came down the shrouds so fast he took the skin off both hands.

He did not go to Ignacio. He went to Brites, which told everybody something.

"It is not in my ears," he said.

"Where is it."

He put his hand flat on his own breastbone, and then, not finding that enough, he knocked on it twice with his knuckles. "Here. It is in the bone. It is in my teeth. I can hear the ship and the wind at the same time and it is *under* them." His face had gone the colour of the sand down there. "I thought it was a story. I have been telling people it was a story. I am sorry, captain."

Nobody spoke. Fausto had come up from below with his hat on and was holding the mizzen shrouds with both hands and looking at the boy the way a man looks at a bill.

And Ignacio found that the thing he felt was not triumph, and was not fear, and had no name he knew: eleven years of being the only person aboard any ship who believed it, ended, in the time it took a boy to knock on his own chest. There was nothing left to argue about. There was only the work.

"Both boats down," he said. "Hair-rope in the bow of each. Brites, the second boat is yours."

Brites went to the rail, unpicked the plait she had worn for fifteen years, cut it off at the nape with the knife she never used for anything, and handed it forward to be woven in.

"Why?" Pau said.

"Because we are short," she said, and her hands were not steady, and she did not say why they were not.

The method was eleven years old and had never once been used on anything.

Two boats, a hundred feet apart, the hair-rope bent between them with lead on it to make it sink, and the whole arrangement rowed slowly up a weed lane with the bottom in plain sight the whole way. Whatever crossed the lane crossed the rope. That was all of it. Ignacio had explained it to three financiers and it had sounded better each time.

What he had not allowed for, in eleven years of explaining, was that the men pulling the oars would be inside the song.

It began about the fourth stroke. Pau, in the stern of the first boat with the line, said, "there," in an ordinary voice, as if he had spotted a church on a headland. Then the two oarsmen were no longer rowing the same water. Ignacio watched the second boat's bow come round three points to port with nobody in it choosing to do that, and the rope between them went slack, and he heard himself shouting at men who could hear him perfectly well and simply did not find him interesting.

"Off the line!" Brites had got hold of the loom of the nearest oar with both hands. "Robles, we are off the line, we are going to foul the ribs of that wreck—"

Then she was in the water.

Nobody saw how. That is the truth of it and it went into the log that way. One moment she had the oar and the next there was a white gap where she had been and the boat rocked once.

Eleven seconds. Ignacio counted them because counting was the only work available to him, and at eleven her hand came up on the far side of the boat, not the side she had gone in, and two men had her by the wrist and the collar and she came over the gunwale streaming and coughing salt.

She sat in the bottom of the boat for a while. The song had stopped. Everyone had noticed that it had stopped, and nobody said so.

"Well?" said Ignacio.

Brites opened her mouth and shut it again.

"You were under. What did you see?"

"I do not know," she said. "Captain, I was looking straight at it and I do not know."

## 2. The Half-Hour Glass

They went back down the lane at slack water with the rope re-led and every man in both boats lashed to his thwart by the belt, which was Brites's idea and was not discussed.

The second cast held.

It held the way a line holds a fish that is much bigger than the line: not a jerk but a long steady taking, the hair-rope singing up out of the water in a straight cord of drops, the second boat going down by the bow until Pau had both feet on the stemhead and his weight out over the water. Then it came up.

Three turns had taken her at the waist, and rule five was true, and she did not break them.

They got her into the bottom of the boat. She was smaller than eleven years of stories had made her, and the grey came into her skin while they watched, like breath going onto a window, and her eyes had no white in them anywhere. She did not thrash. She looked at each of them in turn, at the two oarsmen, at Pau, at Brites, and she made a sound that was not in the air but in the breastbone, once, low, and every man in the boat wept without changing his face, which is a thing Ignacio never afterwards found the words for.

He knelt down in the bilge water to take hold of the rope.

And he saw her left forearm.

The tear ran from the wrist to the inside of the elbow, healed long ago, white against the shale colour, and it was the arm that had gone down past his leg in flat bright water on a morning eleven years before, and it was the arm that had held him up under the shoulder for a night and a day while he did not tread water and did not float.

His hands came off the rope.

"Captain," said Brites.

Nothing. He was looking at the arm.

"Ignacio."

"Get her in the tank," he said, and his voice came out level, which frightened Pau more than anything else in the whole voyage. "Fill it from over the side. Go."

And behind them, at the ship's rail, quite calm, Don Fausto Delgado took the half-hour glass off its nail, turned it, and said: "From now. Somebody write down the hour."

The tank was eight feet by four and lead-lined and had cost more than the rest of the ship, and Ignacio had drawn it himself on the back of a chart nine years before, and in six days he went down to it twice.

Pau went down to it eleven times a day.

The water had to be changed, because seawater in a lead box goes stale in a day and you can smell when it has. That meant buckets from over the side, and the grating off, and a boy kneeling on the beam over the open top with his long hair hanging down into the lamplight. Fausto had written a rule about the grating. Pau kept to it for two days.

The glass was nailed to the beam where everybody going aft had to pass it, and the tally went on the beam beside it in chalk, one stroke to a turn, five and a gate. By the sixth day the gates ran the whole length of the beam and had started up the knee of the frame.

At thirty turns she sang, and you had to go on deck to get away from it, and half the watch slept forward with their fingers in their ears knowing perfectly well that fingers were no use.

At a hundred she sang lower and not often.

At two hundred and forty there was no singing. There was a sound like a person breathing out on purpose, once, when the lamp came near, and Pau found he could not hear it in the bone any more, only in his ears, which he understood to be the wrong way round and worse.

He sat on the beam with the empty bucket and said what he had been carrying for three days.

"Captain."

Ignacio was on the ladder. He had come down as far as the ladder, which was as far as he had come since the fifth day.

"Sir, I want to ask a thing and then I will not ask it again." The boy did not turn round. "We have her. Nobody can say she is a story now, because she is in the hold and I have my hands wet from her water. So what is the proving for? Who is it for, sir? Because it is not for you and it is not for me, and she is spending herself to pay for it."

The ladder creaked twice and then did not creak again, and when Pau looked round the captain had gone up.

On the seventh evening Don Fausto came up with the ledger, unlocked it on the capstan head, and laid the bond out with the third clause uppermost, the way he always did, like a man putting down a chart.

"A place of the financier's choosing," he read. "Witnesses of the financier's choosing. I have chosen. There is a hall behind the Lonja in Seville that I have had the use of since March, and eleven men who will sit in it, four of them of the sort a court will listen to."

"Seville is up the river," said Brites.

"It is."

"We cannot take her up the river." She said it flatly, the way she said the price of things. "Rule seven. Nothing has ever been brought over the bar alive and nothing ever will be. That is not a saying, Delgado, it is a count of dead ones."

"It is a count of dead *fish*," Fausto said, without heat, without raising his voice at all. "Taken by men in open boats with no tank, in summer, and dead before they came within a league of the water changing. I have built a lead tank. I have a boy who empties it eleven times a day. I am not a superstitious man and I will not be governed by a rule that has never once been tested by anybody who could afford to test it properly." He closed the ledger. "Captain. Course for the Guadalquivir bar. We stand in at first light."

Ignacio stood at the rail with his back to both of them and turned the glass over under his shirt.

He said nothing.

Fausto took that for what he wanted it to be and went below.

Brites waited until the hatch was shut.

"Say the price to me, then," she said, "since you will not say it to him. Four days to the bar. Two days up the river with her over the sill of the fresh water. She will be dead before the hall, and if she is not dead she will be a grey thing in a box that eleven honest men look at and afterwards describe eleven different ways, because that is rule four and the tank does not beat rule four either." She let that sit. "You will have spent her and bought nothing. Robles. Give me an order."

He gave her none.

At first light Pau raised the bar from the masthead: a brown seam across green water, half a mile wide, dead ahead.

## 3. The Brown Line

The bar looked like nothing. That was the part nobody who tells this story ever gets right. It was a brown seam lying across green water half a mile off the bow, no surf on it, no noise, a change of colour you could have sailed over asleep.

"Helm up," said Fausto. "Stand in."

Brites took her hands off the tiller and stepped back from it, one clear pace, and put them behind her.

"No," she said.

"Olaya—"

"I have taken this ship where Robles said for eleven years and half of it was madness and I went anyway, because it was his madness and he paid for it out of his own hide." She was looking at Ignacio, not at Fausto. "This is not his. This is a hall behind the Lonja with eleven chairs in it. I will not put her over that line. Take the helm yourself."

Fausto took the helm himself. He was sixty and seasick and he had been at the tiller of a river boat as a boy, and he held the ship straight at the seam and did it competently, and that was somehow the worst thing that had happened yet.

Ignacio came aft.

He had the bond in his hand. He did not tear it, and everybody aboard was expecting him to tear it, and afterwards Pau said that was the moment he understood the captain properly for the first time: the man folded it back along its own creases and put it in Fausto's coat pocket for him.

"Keep it," he said. "The ship is yours in seventeen days and I will not argue the count."

"Captain—"

"I have been trying to be believed since I was twenty years old." He said it the way he said a bearing. "Eleven years, three commissions, nine pounds of other people's hair. And I have got her, Don Fausto, and I have had her six days, and there is no arrangement of chairs in Seville that will make one man's word into two men's word. Rule four does not care about your hall. It never did. I am not trying any more."

He put his hand on the tiller over the old man's hand and put it down.

"Hard a-starboard. Southwest by west. Every stitch she will carry."

"That is back to the shoal," said Pau.

"Four days back to the shoal," said Ignacio. "Go and count the beam and tell me what we have left of her."

They raised the Restinga on the fourth morning in a glass calm, which is the worst weather for making a passage and the only weather in which you can see the bottom of nine miles of shoal at once.

Sand. The weed lanes going away west. The black ribs of the ship nobody ever named.

They got her up out of the tank in a sail and she weighed nothing, and the grey had gone all through her, and the tear on the left forearm was the one thing about her that had not changed since the morning Ignacio was twenty years old.

Brites had the knife out.

"Give it here," he said.

"Captain, I will do it."

"You cut your hair for this rope," he said. "I will cut the rope."

So it was Ignacio Robles who knelt on the chainplates over two fathoms of bright water with the whole crew standing off behind him, and took the three turns at her waist in his left hand, and put the knife under them.

He said something first. Pau was four feet away and heard it and would not repeat it to anybody in Cadiz, then or later, and when he was an old man in Sanlucar he still would not, except to say that it was short and that it was not a prayer.

The hair-rope parted at the second draw.

She went over the side without being helped, and she did not sing, because there was nothing left to sing with, and that was the price and it had been paid by the wrong person and everybody on deck knew it.

Two fathoms down she turned once in the light and her arm came past with the white tear on it, the way it had come past his leg eleven years ago, and then she was a shape over the sand, and then she was the sand.

Nobody spoke for a long time.

The shoal lay there in the calm, bright as a plate, entirely empty, nine miles of it, with the bottom in plain sight the whole way across and nothing whatever on it to show that any of this had ever happened.

"Well," Fausto said at last, from the mizzen shrouds, in a voice that had lost all its dryness. "And now not one soul will believe a word of it."

"No," said Ignacio. "They will not."

He stood up off his knees and coiled the two cut ends of the rope and handed them to the boy, and went forward to see about the ship.

La Alondra came into Cadiz seven days past her bond, out of a calm that had held her a fortnight off Trafalgar, with an empty hold and a lead tank of stale water nobody had emptied, and the news was all over the fish quay before the warps were on the ring.

It went the way such news goes.

The rope-walk woman said she had sold him nine pounds of hair and that was the only fact in it. A man off a Sanlucar boat said the captain of the Alondra had gone mad in the Gulf and thrown a woman overboard, and was made to take it back, and took it back. Two of the crew told it straight in three taverns and were bought drinks for it, which is what a town does to a story it has decided about. By Lent it had become a thing men said to each other about Ignacio Robles when he was out of the room, and the shape it had was: *eleven years, and nothing to show.*

Which was exact, and he never once disputed it.

Fausto Delgado took the ship, as he was entitled to, and sold her to somebody who fished, as he had said he would. He never spoke of the voyage in Seville or anywhere else; the hall behind the Lonja was let to a man selling Flemish glass. That is all anybody knows about what Don Fausto made of it, and it is probably not nothing.

Brites Olaya went first mate into a Genoa trader and grew her plait back, and would answer any question about the voyage except one.

And Pau kept the rope.

He had the two cut ends of it in a tin box for sixty years, and he showed it to people, and what they saw was some cut hair in a box, because that is what it was.

They found Ignacio at the head of the slip one evening in the spring, doing nothing at all, looking east past the harbour wall.

"Counting the days, captain?" somebody asked him, meaning it unkindly.

"Three hundred and eleven," he said.

He turned the empty glass over under his shirt. He had no ship, no bond, no witness and no evidence, and rule six is rule six: a year to the day, at the same place. He was not going to be there. He had no way to get there, and he found, sitting on the bollard in the good coat that was too good for a ship he no longer had, that he did not need to go.

She would come up. Whether or not anybody was watching.
