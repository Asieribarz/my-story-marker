# Premise expansion

**Premise (run argument).** A privateer captain who has hunted mermaids for eleven years
finally takes one alive, and finds he has caught the one who once saved him.

## Central conflict

Ignacio Robles has spent eleven years and three commissions trying to prove that the women
in the water are real, because one of them held him up for a night and a day off the
Restinga when he was twenty and should have drowned. Proof requires a body, living or
dead, in front of witnesses who will agree. Against him stands his own patron, Don Fausto
Delgado, who pays for the voyage and owns what it brings back; against him stands the
fourth world rule, which is not a superstition but an observed fact - no two people have
ever seen a mermaid and afterwards agreed on what they saw. And against him, once she is
in the hold, stands the thing he came to prove, losing her voice a half hour at a time.

## Theme

Some things are true and cannot be proved, and a man who insists on the proof destroys the
thing he wanted to be true. Gratitude is owed whether or not anyone will believe the debt.

## Tone, as interpreted for this story

Classic adventure: plain, concrete, forward-moving prose. Rope, water, weather and the
half-hour glass are the real machinery and are never metaphorical. The marvellous is
handled as seamanship - a thing with a range, a season and a working method. No irony, no
winking at the reader. The sea is not cruel; it is simply not interested.

## Tentative ending

Don Fausto means to carry the catch up the river to Seville for a showing, and the seventh
rule says she cannot cross the line where the river water meets the sea. Brites Olaya
refuses the order at the bar. Ignacio cuts the hair-rope himself, in the glass calm over
the Restinga, with what is left of her voice, and lets her go under. He comes home
to Cadiz with an empty hold, an unpaid bond and nothing any court would call evidence. Pau
keeps the cut rope. Nobody believes them, and Ignacio finds he no longer requires it.
