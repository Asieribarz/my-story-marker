Three days out, with the land gone and the ship going along easy on the starboard tack, Brites Olaya came up to the weather rail and stood there long enough that Ignacio knew what was coming.

"Say it to the boy," she said.

"He has heard it."

"He has heard the quay version. Nine men have told him the quay version and no two of them agreed, which is the whole trouble with this ship." She did not look at him. "Say it once, properly, and then I will stop asking."

Pau was at the foot of the mainmast pretending to serve a rope's end. He had given up pretending by the second sentence.

"I was twenty," Ignacio said. "I was on a shoal boat off the Restinga and the boat went over in nothing at all, in flat water, the way they do. I could not swim then and I cannot swim now."

"Go on."

"I was in the water a night and a day. I did not tread it. I did not float. Something held me up under both arms from behind, the whole time, and it breathed, and when the Sanlucar boat came over the edge of the shoal in the morning it let go of me and I saw an arm go down past my leg with a tear across the forearm, here, from the wrist to the elbow, and then there was nothing to see and the bottom was two fathoms down and bright as a plate."

"And the Sanlucar boat," Brites said.

"Four men in it."

"And what did the four men see?"

"One saw a woman. One saw a seal. Two saw me shouting at nothing in flat water, and one of those two is a priest now in Jerez." He turned the glass over under his shirt without knowing he had. "Rule four. Nobody has ever beaten it. That is why I need her aboard and not in anybody's memory."

Pau said, "Captain, what day do we raise the shoal?"

"The nineteenth."

"That is a night and a day from now."

"It is."

"Captain." The boy's voice had gone careful. "The nineteenth is eleven years. To the day."

Brites looked at Ignacio for a long moment, and then she went below without saying anything at all, which from her was the loudest thing available.
