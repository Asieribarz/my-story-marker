The tank was eight feet by four and lead-lined and had cost more than the rest of the ship, and Ignacio had drawn it himself on the back of a chart nine years before, and in six days he went down to it twice.

Pau went down to it eleven times a day.

The water had to be changed, because seawater in a lead box goes stale in a day and you can smell when it has. That meant buckets from over the side, and the grating off, and a boy kneeling on the beam over the open top with his long hair hanging down into the lamplight. Fausto had written a rule about the grating. Pau kept to it for two days.

The glass was nailed to the beam where everybody going aft had to pass it, and the tally went on the beam beside it in chalk, one stroke to a turn, five and a gate. By the sixth day the gates ran the whole length of the beam and had started up the knee of the frame.

At thirty turns she sang, and you had to go on deck to get away from it, and half the watch slept forward with their fingers in their ears knowing perfectly well that fingers were no use.

At a hundred she sang lower and not often.

At two hundred and forty there was no singing. There was a sound like a person breathing out on purpose, once, when the lamp came near, and Pau found he could not hear it in the bone any more, only in his ears, which he understood to be the wrong way round and worse.

He sat on the beam with the empty bucket and said what he had been carrying for three days.

"Captain."

Ignacio was on the ladder. He had come down as far as the ladder, which was as far as he had come since the fifth day.

"Sir, I want to ask a thing and then I will not ask it again." The boy did not turn round. "We have her. Nobody can say she is a story now, because she is in the hold and I have my hands wet from her water. So what is the proving for? Who is it for, sir? Because it is not for you and it is not for me, and she is spending herself to pay for it."

The ladder creaked twice and then did not creak again, and when Pau looked round the captain had gone up.
