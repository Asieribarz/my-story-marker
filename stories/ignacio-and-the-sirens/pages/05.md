They went back down the lane at slack water with the rope re-led and every man in both boats lashed to his thwart by the belt, which was Brites's idea and was not discussed.

The second cast held.

It held the way a line holds a fish that is much bigger than the line: not a jerk but a long steady taking, the hair-rope singing up out of the water in a straight cord of drops, the second boat going down by the bow until Pau had both feet on the stemhead and his weight out over the water. Then it came up.

Three turns had taken her at the waist, and rule five was true, and she did not break them.

They got her into the bottom of the boat. She was smaller than eleven years of stories had made her, and the grey came into her skin while they watched, like breath going onto a window, and her eyes had no white in them anywhere. She did not thrash. She looked at each of them in turn, at the two oarsmen, at Pau, at Brites, and she made a sound that was not in the air but in the breastbone, once, low, and every man in the boat wept without changing his face, which is a thing Ignacio never afterwards found the words for.

He knelt down in the bilge water to take hold of the rope.

And he saw her left forearm.

The tear ran from the wrist to the inside of the elbow, healed long ago, white against the shale colour, and it was the arm that had gone down past his leg in flat bright water on a morning eleven years before, and it was the arm that had held him up under the shoulder for a night and a day while he did not tread water and did not float.

His hands came off the rope.

"Captain," said Brites.

Nothing. He was looking at the arm.

"Ignacio."

"Get her in the tank," he said, and his voice came out level, which frightened Pau more than anything else in the whole voyage. "Fill it from over the side. Go."

And behind them, at the ship's rail, quite calm, Don Fausto Delgado took the half-hour glass off its nail, turned it, and said: "From now. Somebody write down the hour."
