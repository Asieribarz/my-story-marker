# The Salt Road

*An adventure in 10 pages.*


## 1. The Third Season

The Salt Office was swept twice a day and the floor was worn pale in a track from the door to the lectern. Sev Marrow stood at the end of it and watched the factor write.

"Article five." Berec Sund did not look up. "A well-town holds its cistern line only while it lodges a water tally at the Salt Office each season. Three seasons unlodged and the line lapses, when the book closes at noon on the last tally-day, to whoever last drew from it. As the book has it."

Behind him the season's cistern seals hung on the wall in rows, each on its own nail.

"Kethra lodges every season," Sev said.

"Kethra has lodged nothing for two. The third closes at noon on the fifth day, counting this one." He turned the ledger so that she could see the empty column. "You are here about work, guide. There is none."

"I am here about the town."

"The town is a column, as the book has it."

He took down a second book and opened it where it had been opened before.

"Article twelve, the inquiry of eleven years ago. A guide took eleven out on the line and came in alone. The finding was that she left the line to save a night and could not find it again at first light." He read it level, with no weight anywhere in it. "She walked in the next evening off the line, on a bearing and a count of paces. She has never given the count up."

"One," Sev said. "Two. Three."

She did not know she had spoken until the pen stopped.

"Eleven," she said. "Not a finding. Eleven. They have names."

"They have a column also, as the book has it."

She turned for the door and stopped, because she had walked in past it without seeing it.

A child sat against the wall with her knees up and a tin can in both hands. Twelve years old, or near enough. A pale burn round her throat like a collar. The cord at her neck went down inside her shirt, and what she drew up on the end of it was a tally-case with Kethra's seal on it, whole and unbroken. She had come in off the Pan nine days ago on her own legs, the only thing that had come off it this season, and she had no voice to say what was in it.

The standing board was at the head of the ramp where it had been all her life, and there was nothing on it.

Every season there had been a slate hung for each cairn on the line, standing or down, and two more below them for the cisterns at Four and Nine, drawn or sealed. Sev put her hand on the bare frame and counted the empty pegs, and then read the one notice tacked in the corner, which said the board was not posted this season, by order.

"Whose order?"

"It does not say," Oduin Reck said. He did not look at the board. He was looking at the two camels, and he said to them, "She wants to know whose order."

The child had followed Sev out of the Office and stood a little behind her with the can in both hands.

"Two beasts and me," Oduin said. "Out and back. Thirty-one."

"Thirty-one is half what the season is worth."

"Thirty-one is the price."

"Nobody sets half a price in a season nobody is crossing."

"That is a Dray Landing answer," Oduin said, "and I have not given you one." He put the quirt under his arm. "Nine hundred paces of this dock and you will not hear the name of that town once. You know that."

"I know it."

"A quarter of the hands on this ramp walked in off the Pan two seasons ago, out of Kethra, on their own legs, and the Office entered every one of them lapsed. A lapsed man loads salt for what he is given." He put his hand flat on the nearer camel's shoulder. "They do not say it because saying it costs them the day's work. I say it because I am old."

Behind Sev, the child tapped the can twice.

Sev looked at the bare board again, and at the empty pegs, and at the notice in the corner with no name under the word order.

"Then take the thirty-one," she said, "and tell me the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it," Oduin said. He was already turning the beasts. "Except that I am retained by the Office for the season. Have been since the spring."

She waited, and he went on turning the beasts, and he did not say what for.

They came on to Cairn Four a third of the way into the cold and drew the casks full at the slab, and Oduin priced the water aloud while they did it, the way he priced everything. Then two thirds of a window out to Nine, on the child's count, with nothing to see in any direction and the crust ringing under the beasts.

Nine's cone came up in the lamp at the number she had given, and the slab at its foot was off.

Not broken. Lifted, and set down beside the shaft, square to it, the way a man sets a thing down who means to come back for it. The Office seal was not on the slab, and it was not in the shaft, and it was not anywhere.

Sev went down with the lamp. The cistern was the size of a cart and it was drawn out to the floor, dry and clean and swept, with the lining showing and no smell of water in it at all.

She came up and stood in the cold with the sum working in her.

"Four casks. Nine to Kethra is a full window, eighty thousand paces of it. Nine back to Four is two thirds of that. One. Two. Three. Four."

"She is counting," Oduin said, to the camels.

Sev did not hear him. "It is Kethra or it is Four," she said. "It is not both."

"Then it is Four," Oduin said.

"Kethra has three days of book left and a town on the other end of it."

"And we have four casks."

"And a line that gives us nothing until we are standing on the other side of it." Sev put the lamp down on the slab. "I am not turning a party round in the middle of a crossing to sit on the quay and watch a column close. Load."

He looked at her for a while. Then he began to load.

They lay up beside the open shaft through the whole of the day, and the day took its water out of the four casks.

The child had gone down on her heels at the mouth of the shaft. She wrote in the salt with one finger, and what she wrote was eleven, and then she put her flat hand on it and rubbed it out before Oduin had finished reading it over her shoulder.

Eleven days since she drank here, on her way out, alone.

The light came up behind them and Kethra stood out of the white all at once, forty flat roofs on the only rise there was.

Nothing moved on it. They came up through the ring of houses with the sun a finger off the crust and the beasts going short, and Sev saw what was wrong before she had a word for it. Every door in the town was barred on the outside. Every one. The bars were in their brackets and the brackets were sound and the dust lay even along the tops of them.

"Nobody does that to a town," Oduin said, to the camels. "A town does that to itself, going out."

The windlass stood over the wellhead with its rope coiled and made up. Sev dropped a stone down it and the well answered wrong: not the fall and the knock of a dry shaft, and not water either, but a flat slap close under her, like a hand laid on a table.

The child was already going along the wall to the tally house.

It was one room against the wellhead. The keeper was at the table with his back to the door and his head down on his arm, and he had been there some days, and there was a cup by his hand with a white crust dried in the bottom of it.

Sev did not touch him. She went round the table to see the book.

The ledger lay open under his other arm at the foot of a page, and the last thing written in it was three lines in a steady hand, sworn on such a day, the apprentice of this town's count, and then the name of the child standing beside her.

Under it the season's column was ruled and empty.

He had gone on drinking it. She had stopped.

"Where is the tally?" Sev said.

The child put her hand flat on the case inside her shirt and held it there.


## 2. The Count

"Do not," Sev said.

The child had the cord over her head before the word was out of her. She set the case on the table by the keeper's arm, held it with one hand, and broke the seal against the edge with the other. It went in three pieces, and the pieces of it lay there, and the year and the Office stamp were in two of them.

"Article five," Sev said. "A case broken anywhere but on the Office lectern is void as a lodgement. You have just made that thing worth nothing at the one place it was worth anything."

The child turned the case over and shook it.

Nothing came out of it. She put her finger in and drew it round the inside and held the finger up, and there was salt dust on it and that was all. No folded sheet. No strip. No count.

"Thirty-one," Oduin said from the doorway, to the beasts standing outside it, "and four casks, and a crossing, for an empty box."

Sev stood with her hands on the table and made herself go slowly.

The keeper had sworn an apprentice and written nothing under it. The town had gone out on its own legs and the well had gone to brine. The case had come out sealed and had come out empty, and the child had carried it eighty miles and sat nine days against a wall with it, and had never once let anybody open it.

"It is not in the case," Sev said. "It is in you."

The child looked round the room. Her town. Her keeper. Her table, and the bars on every door of the forty houses she had walked eighty miles to come back to.

"And it is worth nothing until it is at that lectern by noon on the fifth day," Sev said. "So you do not stay. You have been home half a day and you go out on to it again tonight."

"She is twelve," Oduin said.

"I know what she is."

The child did not nod. She looked at Sev for a long moment, and then she turned back to the table and wrote it out plainly in the salt dust with one finger: that the keeper had sent the case out empty because the last two tallies had never reached the lectern, and that the case was the thing he meant them to take.

The tally-house floor came up in three boards and the town's own cistern was under it, sealed, the year and the Office stamp whole in the wax.

Oduin measured it by eye. "One crossing," he said. "As far as Four. Not a swallow past it."

"Then we open Four," Sev said.

They drew it and roped the casks, and while they worked the child sat at the table with the ledger turned round to her and wrote.

She wrote in the ruled column under the empty space where the season should have been. Date. Cairn. Quantity. Her hand was small and square and did not stop to think about any of it.

Oduin stood behind her with a rope over his shoulder, read six lines of it, and put the rope down.

"Those are not what Kethra took."

"It is Kethra's count," Sev said.

"I know what Kethra took. Four barrels a season and hard years between." He put one finger on the boards beside the ledger, not on the page. "That is what came off the line. I loaded it at the ramp head in the spring, under a stamp, with the board bare so nobody could read what was drawn and what was sealed."

Sev waited.

"Forty," Oduin said. "From the Office. In the spring. Not to lose you and not to drown you. To take my time about it, and to bring you in on the wrong side of noon, and be a slow old man with two slow beasts."

They finished the loading in the dark, and barred the tally-house door on the outside the way the town had barred every other one, and took the beasts down the one street and out on to the crust at full dark, with the child's count already running. The child had Kethra's ledger under her arm. Nobody told her to leave it.

A hundred paces out, Oduin stopped the line.

He came back down it to her with the coin in his hand, the only bright thing on the Pan.

"Write my name," he said to the child. "Under the count, where the Office can read it. I have carried it since the spring and would rather have it in a book."

The child set the ledger on a cask, made a space and wrote his name in it, and he dropped the coin into her can to seal it, and it rattled there for the rest of the crossing.

They lay up through the middle of the day under the tarpaulin with the beasts couched and the crust too hot to put a flat hand on, and drank what the day cost them and not a mouthful more. Nine had come and gone in the night behind them, dry, the slab still lying square beside its shaft where nobody had moved it.

At full dark they got up and went on, two thirds of a window to Cairn Four, on the child's count.

There is nothing to say about that walk. The lamp reached twenty paces and the twenty paces were white, and beyond them was the same white without the lamp on it, and the only thing in the world that knew where they were was a girl with no voice walking at the near shoulder of the first camel.

Sev counted aloud from the halfway of it and did not know she was doing it. Oduin heard her and said nothing, which was a kindness.

Then the child stopped.

She stood with the can in both hands and she did not go on, and after a moment she put one foot forward and set it down flat, twice, on the place.

Sev brought the lamp round. Twenty paces of white. No cone. No whitewash taking the light back. No slab, no shadow, no heap of blocks the height of a standing man.

"Count it again."

The child tapped the can once.

"Could you be short a thousand? It is a long stage and you are carrying."

The child went down and wrote the number in the salt, the same number, and left it there.

"Could the wind have set you off the line in the dark? It has done it to better than you."

Nothing at all. No tap, no hand, no writing. She stood on the place she had put her foot on and looked at Sev and waited for Sev to be finished.

Under all three of them, very faintly, the crust began to tick, the way it does when the cold is going out of it.


## 3. Off the Line

First light came up ahead of them and went out along the crust the way it always does, and the whitewash on Cairn Four's landing side took it before anything else on the Pan.

It did not. There was nothing out there to take it. White to the edge of sight, and the ridges throwing their little shadows, and no cone anywhere in any part of it.

Oduin said, "Then she is off the line."

"She is not off the line."

"That is a Dray Landing answer," Oduin said. "Then the cairn has walked."

"Yes," Sev said. "It has."

She stood and let the sun come, which was the one thing she could not afford, and made herself think it through instead of feeling it.

"There is a thing out there," she said. "Off the line, north of it, between Four and Three. I know what bearing it lies on from Four and I know how many paces, because I walked it the other way once in the morning, and I have had the number in my head every day since."

Oduin waited.

"It is my caravan," she said. "It is eleven people. I took them off the line to save a night and I could not find the line again at first light, and that is where they are."

She picked the beasts up and put them on the bearing before anyone could answer her.

The crust was already going. It let them through to the ankle in the low places and the camels hated it, and behind them the line they had been counting on for two nights stopped being anything at all, because a cairn you cannot see is a cairn you cannot go back to.

It came up out of the white after nineteen hundred paces, the way it must have stood for eleven years: a stack of cargo salted over into one grey block, ropes still on it, a guide's tally-cord knotted to the rope where she had left it, the mate of the one on her own belt.

Sev did not stop at it. She took the bearing off it backwards, and set the child at her shoulder to count, and walked the nineteen hundred paces in again.

The count ended on a slab, with Cairn Four's stamp still under the salt on it.

The slab took the three of them and a rope to shift, and the shaft under it was dark and cold and smelled of water, and the seal on the cistern below was whole, with the year and the Office stamp in the wax.

They drank standing at the shaft mouth. Then they filled what they could carry, and set the slab back over the shaft and weighted it, because a cistern left open is brine inside the day.

By then the sun was a hand up and the crust had begun to let go. The nearer camel went in to the knee at the second step and stood there with its head down, not fighting it, which is worse.

Oduin looked at that for a while.

Then he went and cut the loads off both beasts and let them down into the salt, and turned the camels loose, light, and put them away from him towards the landing with the flat of his hand.

"They will come in or they will not," he said. "They will not come in carrying."

Nobody priced it.

They went the rest of the way on their own legs, which is the only thing that moves on the Pan after sunrise, wet to the knee and then wet to the thigh, with the child between them and the water on their backs. It took the whole of the morning. Twice the crust let Sev through to the hip and twice Oduin had her out of it before she had finished going.

The dock hands saw them first, a long way out, and came down to the foot of the ramp and stood there, twenty or thirty of them, and did not shout or ask anything. They were the ones who had walked in off the Pan out of Kethra two seasons ago and been entered lapsed, and they knew what it looked like from the inside.

Somebody took the casks. Somebody put a coat on the child.

Sev went up the ramp with an hour of the morning left and pushed the Office door open.

Behind the lectern the Kethra nail was empty, and the only paper on the board under it was an order striking Cairn Four from the line for repair, dated the night they went out.

"Article five," Berec Sund said. "A tally-case broken anywhere but on this lectern is void as a lodgement. As the book has it."

"It is," Sev said. "She broke it. It was empty when she broke it."

"Then there is nothing to lodge, and article nine. A tally is given by the keeper of the town." He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. "This town has no keeper."

The child set Kethra's ledger on the lectern and turned it round to him, open at the last entry: three lines in a steady hand, sworn on such a day, the apprentice of this town's count, and a name under it.

"It has," Sev said.

"It has a keeper who cannot give a tally."

"Show me the article that says she gives it with her mouth."

Sund looked at the ledger. "Give it, then," he said, in the voice of a man asked to wait for something that cannot happen.

The child took the pen.

A keeper's count is not what a town drinks but what comes off the line: date, cairn, quantity, and the hand that drew it. She wrote it down the season's column in her own hand. Kethra, four barrels a season, hard years between. Then, under the Office stamp, from the spring on, load after load, out of Nine until there was no Nine left.

Sev took the Kethra seals off their nails and laid them along the lectern beside the column. There were four of them. The column ran to two pages.

Nobody had to explain it to the men in the doorway. They had walked in off the Pan out of that town and been entered lapsed, and they could read a column.

Article five gives a lapsed line to whoever last drew from it. The count said who that was, in the Office's own numbers and under its own stamp, and the book had not closed, so nothing had lapsed.

Sund entered the lodgement himself. He took the ring off its cord to stamp it and left ink along the edge of his hand the way he always did, and by the afternoon the Office had another factor.

Sev signed beneath the count. Then she wrote eleven names under her own, and Oduin's under those.

The line was Kethra's, and Kethra's keeper was twelve years old, and she gave its cairns and its two cisterns to Sev to keep. Sev went out on to the Pan in the cold that night to build Cairn Four up again.
