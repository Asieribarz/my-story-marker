First light came up ahead of them and went out along the crust the way it always does, and the whitewash on Cairn Four's landing side took it before anything else on the Pan.

It did not. There was nothing out there to take it. White to the edge of sight, and the ridges throwing their little shadows, and no cone anywhere in any part of it.

Oduin said, "Then she is off the line."

"She is not off the line."

"That is a Dray Landing answer," Oduin said. "Then the cairn has walked."

"Yes," Sev said. "It has."

She stood and let the sun come, which was the one thing she could not afford, and made herself think it through instead of feeling it.

"There is a thing out there," she said. "Off the line, north of it, between Four and Three. I know what bearing it lies on from Four and I know how many paces, because I walked it the other way once in the morning, and I have had the number in my head every day since."

Oduin waited.

"It is my caravan," she said. "It is eleven people. I took them off the line to save a night and I could not find the line again at first light, and that is where they are."

She picked the beasts up and put them on the bearing before anyone could answer her.

The crust was already going. It let them through to the ankle in the low places and the camels hated it, and behind them the line they had been counting on for two nights stopped being anything at all, because a cairn you cannot see is a cairn you cannot go back to.

It came up out of the white after nineteen hundred paces, the way it must have stood for eleven years: a stack of cargo salted over into one grey block, ropes still on it, a guide's tally-cord knotted to the rope where she had left it, the mate of the one on her own belt.

Sev did not stop at it. She took the bearing off it backwards, and set the child at her shoulder to count, and walked the nineteen hundred paces in again.

The count ended on a slab, with Cairn Four's stamp still under the salt on it.
