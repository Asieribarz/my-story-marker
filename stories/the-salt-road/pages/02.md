The standing board was at the head of the ramp where it had been all her life, and there was nothing on it.

Every season there had been a slate hung for each cairn on the line, standing or down, and two more below them for the cisterns at Four and Nine, drawn or sealed. Sev put her hand on the bare frame and counted the empty pegs, and then read the one notice tacked in the corner, which said the board was not posted this season, by order.

"Whose order?"

"It does not say," Oduin Reck said. He did not look at the board. He was looking at the two camels, and he said to them, "She wants to know whose order."

The child had followed Sev out of the Office and stood a little behind her with the can in both hands.

"Two beasts and me," Oduin said. "Out and back. Thirty-one."

"Thirty-one is half what the season is worth."

"Thirty-one is the price."

"Nobody sets half a price in a season nobody is crossing."

"That is a Dray Landing answer," Oduin said, "and I have not given you one." He put the quirt under his arm. "Nine hundred paces of this dock and you will not hear the name of that town once. You know that."

"I know it."

"A quarter of the hands on this ramp walked in off the Pan two seasons ago, out of Kethra, on their own legs, and the Office entered every one of them lapsed. A lapsed man loads salt for what he is given." He put his hand flat on the nearer camel's shoulder. "They do not say it because saying it costs them the day's work. I say it because I am old."

Behind Sev, the child tapped the can twice.

Sev looked at the bare board again, and at the empty pegs, and at the notice in the corner with no name under the word order.

"Then take the thirty-one," she said, "and tell me the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it," Oduin said. He was already turning the beasts. "Except that I am retained by the Office for the season. Have been since the spring."

She waited, and he went on turning the beasts, and he did not say what for.
