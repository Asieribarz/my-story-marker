The tally-house floor came up in three boards and the town's own cistern was under it, sealed, the year and the Office stamp whole in the wax.

Oduin measured it by eye. "One crossing," he said. "As far as Four. Not a swallow past it."

"Then we open Four," Sev said.

They drew it and roped the casks, and while they worked the child sat at the table with the ledger turned round to her and wrote.

She wrote in the ruled column under the empty space where the season should have been. Date. Cairn. Quantity. Her hand was small and square and did not stop to think about any of it.

Oduin stood behind her with a rope over his shoulder, read six lines of it, and put the rope down.

"Those are not what Kethra took."

"It is Kethra's count," Sev said.

"I know what Kethra took. Four barrels a season and hard years between." He put one finger on the boards beside the ledger, not on the page. "That is what came off the line. I loaded it at the ramp head in the spring, under a stamp, with the board bare so nobody could read what was drawn and what was sealed."

Sev waited.

"Forty," Oduin said. "From the Office. In the spring. Not to lose you and not to drown you. To take my time about it, and to bring you in on the wrong side of noon, and be a slow old man with two slow beasts."

They finished the loading in the dark, and barred the tally-house door on the outside the way the town had barred every other one, and took the beasts down the one street and out on to the crust at full dark, with the child's count already running. The child had Kethra's ledger under her arm. Nobody told her to leave it.

A hundred paces out, Oduin stopped the line.

He came back down it to her with the coin in his hand, the only bright thing on the Pan.

"Write my name," he said to the child. "Under the count, where the Office can read it. I have carried it since the spring and would rather have it in a book."

The child set the ledger on a cask, made a space and wrote his name in it, and he dropped the coin into her can to seal it, and it rattled there for the rest of the crossing.
